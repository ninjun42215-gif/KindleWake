# KindleWake — In-Depth Lore Bible
## The Sundered Vow

**Document role:** Canonical creative reference for the world, history, factions, characters, territories, supernatural rules and narrative tone.  
**Status:** Consolidated canon and story direction. Specific facts already established in the GDD are canon; additional details are framed as design direction until authored into game data and scenes.

---

## 1. The heart of the story

KindleWake is a story about **the people left behind when institutions fail**. It is not simply a tale of reclaiming a throne, avenging a murdered father or defeating an evil regent. It asks what kind of peace deserves to survive, who has paid for it, and whether a realm founded on a broken promise can be repaired without repeating the same abuses.

The player is the last surviving heir of House Dunmarrow. Their father, Edmer Dunmarrow, was executed as a traitor. The family estate burned, the household was scattered and the truth was buried beneath official accounts. The heir inherits a ruined fortress, Kaer Sethorn, a small company of witnesses and fragments of a sealed oath.

The campaign begins as a struggle to survive and protect a few people. It expands into a struggle over memory, legitimacy, land, food, law, faith, military power and the future of the realm.

**The emotional promise:** the player should come to care about a company of flawed people, see their home recover, and feel the consequences of choices in dialogue, relationships, battles, territory and the final chronicle.

## 2. The Covenant of Seven Flames

The Covenant of the Seven Flames is first and foremost the ancient public compact: a political and moral agreement that bound the Seven Houses and their recognised communities to mutual obligations. It is not a magical relic, a royal title or a secret society. Its surviving seals and the Hidden Codicil are documentary/oath-bearing instruments associated with it. Separately, the live faction data contains a secret organisation named covenant_seven_flames (The Covenant of the Seven Flames). That secret order is a later, distinct organisation which adopted the compact's name; it is not the original compact, not the Seven Houses, and not proof that every person who honours the public Covenant belongs to the order. Keep dialogue, quests, faction relations and records explicit about which meaning is intended.

The Seven Houses publicly claim that the Covenant protects the realm. Over generations, each house preserved a different fragment and edited or ignored obligations that became inconvenient. The Hidden Codicil records the original terms, including:
- shared grain reserves during famine;
- limits on forced conscription;
- sanctuary for displaced families;
- a binding right of appeal for common towns;
- mutual obligations between rulers and the people whose labour sustains them.

The Seven Flames are therefore both a symbol of unity and evidence of institutional betrayal. The seven seals/fragments should connect the continents, but they must not erase the local histories and conflicts of each region.

The Covenant's supernatural dimension should remain restrained and costly. Magic is rare and feared. A seal can preserve, reveal, bind or witness an oath; it should not become a convenient answer to every political or personal problem. The exact metaphysics, costs and limits of each flame must be authored consistently before implementation.

## 2.1 Political chronology and constitutional terms

This chronology reconciles the original GDD's King Osric IV and Crownlands setting with the later Last Emperor ending. It is the canon reference for all future story writing.

1. **The old Eldrimar Empire:** In an earlier age, an imperial order linked the central Crownlands to a wider network of roads, charters and allied realms. The Covenant of the Seven Flames emerged as a compact of mutual obligations among seven great houses and the communities under their protection. It was never simply an imperial religion or a magical artefact.
2. **The imperial fragmentation:** The old Empire fractured over generations. Its central authority ceased to govern the whole known world, and its former territories developed distinct crowns, assemblies, compacts and local laws. The Eldrimar Crownlands survived as a successor monarchy. The imperial office became dormant rather than an active throne; old imperial charters and legal procedures survived in fragments.
3. **The Crownlands under Osric IV:** At the opening of the game, Osric IV is King of the Crownlands, not a reigning Emperor over all seven continents. Aurelmont is the Crownlands capital. The six other recognised noble houses alongside Dunmarrow are political powers within Eldrimar, not automatically vassals of a functioning world empire.
4. **The succession crisis:** Osric dies without a secure heir. Lord Regent Maelor Brennhallow exercises authority in the Crownlands. Edmer Dunmarrow, keeper of the Hidden Codicil, is executed and the Dunmarrow estate is burned. The Codicil exposes the Covenant's original obligations and directs the Crownlands' single-crown authority to be transferred to a Council of the Seven Houses. It provides a lawful route for resolving the Crownlands crisis; it does not itself instantly make every realm part of a new empire.
5. **The Last Emperor settlement:** Only in the designated true-ending route, after the Council of Seven has been constituted under the Codicil and the necessary house/realm ratifications secured, is the dormant imperial constitutional office temporarily reconstituted. The Council invests the protagonist as **Last Emperor** for one narrow purpose: to wind down the surviving imperial claims and institutions, complete the final settlement and transfer authority to the Concord under the surviving charters. This office does not reverse the Council's dissolution of the Crownlands' single crown. It is a transitional legal mandate, not a conquest of every continent and not a permanent restoration of imperial supremacy.
6. **Dissolution and the Concord:** The Last Emperor formally dissolves the imperial office and central imperial claim, records the dissolution charter and establishes the Concord of Free Realms among willing signatories. Each realm retains its own laws and may refuse, contest or later renegotiate the Concord. No successor may ordinarily inherit or purchase the title of Emperor.

**Terminology:** use *King of the Crownlands* for Osric IV; *Lord Regent* for Maelor during the opening crisis; *the old Eldrimar Empire* for the historical imperial order; *Last Emperor* only for the protagonist's temporary endgame office; and *Concord of Free Realms* for the post-dissolution compact. Do not call Osric an Emperor, describe Maelor as Regent of a functioning seven-continent empire, or imply the protagonist begins the story as a prince/emperor.

## 3. The Dunmarrow fall

House Dunmarrow once held an important place in Eldrimar's political order. Edmer Dunmarrow was executed after intending to expose the broken Covenant and its Hidden Codicil. His accusation of treason became the official story.

The fire at the estate destroyed lives, records and the family's public standing. The player-character survives as the heir, but is not automatically a rightful saviour. They possess an inherited name, incomplete memories and a small group of witnesses—not unquestionable moral authority.

The central antagonist, Regent Maelor Brennhallow, suppressed the Codicil because he believes exposing the truth during a succession crisis will trigger famine, invasion and civil war. His fear is credible; his methods are indefensible. He must remain a political actor with arguments, allies, compromises and consequences—not a secretly evil caricature.

## 4. The opening and established characters

### Torren Dunmarrow — the player character
Torren is the canonical protagonist identity and stable character record (protagonist_id: torren). The character-creation screen customises the playable Torren's name, presentation, face, hair, skin, build, origin, discipline, weapon preference and temperament; it does not create a second, unrelated heir alongside a non-player Torren. The default profile is Torren Dunmarrow. Narrative references to Torren, the surviving heir and the player-character therefore refer to the same protagonist unless a scene explicitly discusses a historical account or an alias. Keep the stable internal ID torren even when the player changes the displayed name or appearance. His arc moves from “I must restore what was taken” toward “I must decide what deserves to be restored.” His defining flaw is confusing duty with self-erasure.

### Bram Vosk
A veteran who values practical protection over clean hands. He embodies the cost of loyalty and must learn that loyalty can include disagreement rather than obedience.

### Sela Wrenfield
A scout who understands how ordinary people survive official stories. She begins as a distrustful witness and can grow into someone willing to be publicly accountable for what she knows.

### Brother Wystan
A man of faith who has not lost belief, but has lost certainty that the powerful speak for it. His arc distinguishes mercy from surrender.

### Edmer Dunmarrow
The executed keeper of the household legacy. His actions must be revealed in layers. He should not be made flawless: the Codicil material already establishes that he concealed at least one clause to protect someone.

### Regent Maelor Brennhallow
The antagonist whose stated fear is that truth released at the wrong moment will bring famine, invasion and civil war. Give him moments where his policies save lives as well as moments where they destroy them. His strongest argument should be heard before the final choice.

### Recruited company
A recruit is a person, not a roster slot. Every named recruit needs a want, a fear, a contradiction, a voice, a relationship pressure point, a reason to stay, a personal quest and a battle identity. Loyalty must be legible and earned; never trigger arbitrary betrayal solely because a hidden number crossed a threshold.

## 5. Campaign structure

The intended narrative architecture is a prologue plus five acts and an epilogue. These are story promises, not proof that all chapters are playable.

| Chapter | Working title | Core question | Major event / reversal |
|---|---|---|---|
| Prologue | Smoke on the Wind | Escape is not victory | The Dunmarrow estate burns; the player chooses between people, the household record, or splitting the company to attempt both. The record is incomplete and someone altered it before the fire. |
| I | Cold Hearth | Can strangers become a company? | Defend Sallow Crossing's winter stores and decide who receives the last convoy. The raiders were paid to seize a ledger, not food. |
| II | Broken Seals | Does every house remember a different Covenant? | A public hearing at Orrowen and an ambush during a diplomatic escort; a witness recants under pressure against their family. |
| III | The River Campaign | Can territory be won without losing its people? | A multi-stage bridge/river operation; the supposed enemy supply route carries refugees under a false banner. |
| IV | Winter of Knives | Is loyalty the same as agreement? | Infiltrate the Quillon archive while the company fractures; the Codicil reveals Edmer concealed a clause to protect someone. |
| V | The Kindling | Does truth decide the future, or do people? | Multi-front assault on Aurelmont; Maelor offers a genuine settlement if the protagonist surrenders personal power. |
| Epilogue | The Chronicle | What remains after victory? | Return to a rebuilt keep; the company tells the story differently depending on who was heard, lost, forgiven or left behind. |

Each act should follow this rhythm: personal problem → local investigation → tactical operation → irreversible choice → quiet aftermath → new question. Plant evidence before explaining it. Twists should change the meaning of earlier scenes, not erase them.

## 6. The Sevenfold World

The world has seven continents. The global atlas is a campaign overview; it does not imply that every continent is currently explorable.

### Eldrimar — The Sundered Crown
The original political heartland and current opening campaign continent. Its central themes are broken fealty, inheritance, disputed legitimacy, old houses and the cost of civil order. The current detailed playable region is the Sethmarch Frontier. Kaer Sethorn, the Dunmarrow legacy and the Crownlands conflict belong here.

### Thalassar — The Shattered Isles
A maritime continent shaped by sea-charters, harbour powers, tides, trade and contested passage. The first playable coastal slice is Brinegate Coast. Its story concerns the darkened beacon, the Seven Flames oath and the ledger recovered from Tideglass Grotto. Captain Ilyra Venn and pilot Maren Vey are established characters in this regional storyline.

### Varakhan — The Red Expanse
A harsh, expansive land in the established world blueprint. Its identity should be built around survival, routes, resource pressure and local political arrangements—not generic desert tribes. Preserve its authored capital, government, climate, named regions and conflict in `data/continents.json` and related data.

### Nhalor — The Far North
A northern continent in the original world blueprint. Its cold climate and local political identity should create distinctive travel, settlement, military and survival stories. Do not reduce its people to monster-like raiders or a single barbarian stereotype.

### Veyrwood — The Verdant Expanse
A forested continent added in the seven-continent expansion. Its conflicts should reflect competing claims over living land, stewardship, extraction, old paths and the powers that claim to speak for the forest.

### Asterreach — The Stormforged Coast
A storm-shaped coastal continent. Its identity should use weather, seafaring, engineering, trade and the cost of controlling dangerous routes as narrative and gameplay material.

### Ilyr — The Far Lantern
A distant continent connected to the shared Seven Flames mystery. Its identity should be developed through its own people, institutions and conflicts rather than serving only as a lore destination.

**Canon rule:** the exact capitals, governments, climate descriptions, named macro-regions, signature locations and campaign hooks are authored in the live continent data. Copy those records rather than paraphrasing them from memory. Do not rename, merge or invent replacement geography without a deliberate lore revision.

## 7. Political powers and territorial identity

The setting's established political powers include:
- the Crownlands and the Seven Houses of the historical Covenant compact: Dunmarrow, Brennhallow, Ysmark, Quillon, Vessmoor, Tarnholt and Orrowen. The Crownlands monarchy is a separate royal institution, not an eighth house;
- the Tidebound Assembly;
- the Nine Wells Compact;
- the Hearthfjord Jarldoms;
- the Lantern Synod;
- the Thorn Courts;
- the Free Ranger Lodges;
- the Meridian League;
- the Independent Sky Monasteries;
- the public Covenant of the Seven Flames as the historical compact; and, separately, the secret organisation covenant_seven_flames recorded in live faction data. Never conflate the organisation with all Covenant signatories or believers.

Their exact region affiliations and current relations must come from the live faction and continent data. Political borders can change through authored story outcomes. A territory is not just a colour on a map: it has people, food, routes, laws, local leaders, grievances, threats and a reason to resist or cooperate.

The faction system design uses bounded relations from -100 to +100 and diplomatic states such as neutral, non-aggression, trade pact, alliance and war. These values are tools for authored consequences, not a licence for random, unmotivated wars or coups.

## 8. Locations and environmental storytelling

### Kaer Sethorn
The ruined Dunmarrow keep and emotional home base. It should visibly recover through patched banners, missing stones, a half-restored memorial, kitchen smoke, repaired rooms and people returning to work. Facilities should have distinct practical functions and visible upgrades. The burned oath hall, empty memorial wall and blocked well are memorable inspection details in the intended first-hour experience.

### Sallow Crossing
A settlement whose safety and prosperity should visibly respond to the player's actions. Environmental details include flood marks, repaired carts, changing rumours and guards who react to road safety. Its winter stores and convoy decision are central to Chapter I's intended story.

### Orrowen
A location tied to contested land, public hearing and the political meaning of the Covenant. Environmental identity includes coppiced trees, bowyer workshops, trail markers and signs of contested land.

### Vessmoor
A region associated with horse yards, mile stones, weathered duel posts and grain wagons. Its palette is sun-baked ochre, horsehide red and grass gold.

### Quillon
An archive and political knowledge centre. Its palette is ink violet, parchment and muted starlight. Its archive is central to the later revelation about the Hidden Codicil.

### Tarnholt
A sacred/faith-associated location. Its palette is candle ivory, weathered stone and restrained sacred gold. Faith should be represented through human institutions and disagreements, not a simplistic good-versus-corrupt church.

### Brinegate Coast
Brinegate is the seat of the Tidebound Assembly in Thalassar, not the seat of House Ysmark. House Ysmark's seat is explicitly unconfirmed in current faction data and must remain unresolved until an authored reveal establishes it. Do not use the older GDD shorthand “Ysmark Coast (Brinegate, ports)” as a political ownership statement.

The current Thalassar vertical slice: Brinegate harbour, Saltmere, Lantern Quay, Old Beacon Vault, Northstar Wreck and Tideglass Grotto. Its story loop involves Captain Ilyra Venn, the darkened beacon, the grotto, the keeper's strongbox and ledger, the Reef Warden, and the choice to publish the scraped Seven Flames oath or keep it private for a 60-gold patron payment. Maren Vey's recruitment requires both ledger recovery and defeating the grotto guardian.

## 9. Monsters, creatures and enemies — design canon

Enemies must belong to the history, ecology, politics and supernatural rules of the territory where they appear. Avoid arbitrary monster lists pasted onto every region. Use distinct factions and creature families, with visual silhouettes, combat roles, behaviours, drops and encounter context.

**Important:** this section defines the desired content framework. It does not claim every creature below is already implemented. Existing enemy IDs, sprites and stats in the current data files take precedence.

### Human and faction enemies
- Crownland levy soldiers and household guards: disciplined formations, shields, spears, crossbows and morale support.
- House retainers: faction-specific armour, tactics and battlefield doctrine; identify their house by heraldry and equipment.
- Paid road raiders: ambushes, traps, stolen supply wagons and escape behaviour; they may be desperate or hired rather than inherently evil.
- Tidebound privateers and harbour enforcers: nets, hooks, ranged harpoons, boarding tactics and water-edge control.
- Frontier poachers, smugglers and mercenary bands: local knowledge, traps, camouflage and retreat.
- Cult or oath extremists: rare, motivated by a distorted reading of the Covenant; do not make every religious character a villain.

### Eldrimar bestiary direction
- March wolves / roadwolves: pack pressure, flanking and pursuit; tied to frontier roads and the Sallow Marches.
- Marsh hounds and carrion birds: scavengers around flooded roads, battlefields and abandoned settlements.
- Barrow sentinels and oathbound dead: rare undead tied to violated burial vows, sealed tombs and the aftermath of war.
- Ash-wights: ember-scarred remnants around burned estates and fire-damaged ruins; fire and fear interactions.
- Briar boars and thorn-stags: territorial forest wildlife with charge and terrain interactions.
- Ruin scavengers: small opportunistic creatures that flee or swarm around old stonework.

### Thalassar bestiary direction
- Reef lurkers and tide crawlers: ambush from shallow water, resistance to water terrain and vulnerability to dry ground or lightning.
- Salt-wraiths: rare spirits associated with drowned sailors and broken sea oaths; chill, silence or fear effects.
- Reef Warden: established Brinegate guardian/boss identity; its exact implementation is determined by live battle data.
- Storm gulls / cliff raptors: flying harassment, displacement and attacks near cliffs or shipwrecks.
- Wreckbound scavengers: human or creature enemies around the Northstar Wreck; use salvage, cover and narrow deck layouts.

### Varakhan bestiary direction
- Dune stalkers, glass-scaled reptiles, burrowers and carrion swarms: ambush, sand concealment, heat and exposed terrain.
- Caravan breakers and hired outriders: mounted or mobile human threats tied to route control and resource scarcity.
- Ancient seal-wardens: rare guardians of covenant sites, not random dungeon filler.

### Nhalor bestiary direction
- Frost wolves, ice cave prowlers and snow-stalkers: pack hunts, concealment and movement pressure.
- Barrow guardians and oath-dead: tied to northern burial customs and old vows.
- Fjord raiders and rival jarldom warriors: varied motives, ships, shields and terrain-aware tactics.

### Veyrwood bestiary direction
- Thornbound beasts, root hulks and spore swarms: terrain control, poison, entangle and area denial.
- Old-growth wardens: not automatically hostile; conflict may result from trespass, logging or broken agreements.
- Ranger splinter groups and poachers: human conflicts around stewardship and extraction.

### Asterreach bestiary direction
- Storm drakes and cliff wyverns: flying movement, gust displacement and lightning affinity.
- Tide-forged constructs and wreck guardians: mechanical resistance, exposed cores and environmental hazards.
- Smugglers, salvage crews and privateers: tactical encounters around docks, ship decks and storm shelters.

### Ilyr bestiary direction
- Lantern shades and memory-keepers: rare supernatural enemies tied to forgotten testimony and oath memory.
- Far-lantern sentinels: guardians of remote covenant records, with clear rules and weaknesses.
- Local wildlife and political forces must be developed from Ilyr's actual ecology and cultures before adding a large roster.

### Enemy design contract
Every enemy family should specify:
1. territory and habitat;
2. lore explanation and reason for conflict;
3. visual silhouette and sprite/animation source;
4. tactical role (frontline, skirmisher, ranged, controller, support, beast, boss);
5. stats, movement, resistances, weaknesses and abilities;
6. AI behaviour and encounter composition;
7. drops/rewards that fit the ecology and economy;
8. journal entry, bestiary clue or NPC reaction where appropriate;
9. tests for data validity, battle resolution and balance.

Do not use palette swaps as the only form of variety. Reuse existing combat rules and status effects; add new mechanics only when they create a distinct, readable tactical decision.

## 10. Magic, runes and the supernatural

Magic is low-frequency and socially consequential. Most people should not casually cast spectacular spells. Magic users may be feared, regulated, protected or exploited depending on territory.

Runes and sigils should be part of the world's material history and character progression. Existing design includes 77 runes and 11 sigils as recorded project content. Runes may be passive, active, support, stat, unique or heirloom forms. Unique/heirloom runes must retain their protection rules. Fusion, mastery and specialist vendors should have clear in-world explanations and economic costs. Do not let fusion or random drops trivialize the rarity of old oath relics.

## 11. Themes and writing rules

- Duty versus self-erasure.
- Loyalty versus obedience.
- Truth versus stability.
- Mercy versus surrender.
- Inheritance versus earned legitimacy.
- Faith versus institutional power.
- Security versus freedom.
- Home as a place rebuilt by people, not merely upgraded by currency.

Dialogue must be specific to the speaker's knowledge, history and voice. Use subtext, interruption and silence. Do not make every character eloquent or explain lore they would not know. Avoid exposition dumps and generic fantasy declarations. A major twist must be foreshadowed, understandable and emotionally consequential.

## 12. Canon protection checklist

Before adding or changing lore:
- Check the live continent, faction, character, item, quest and enemy data.
- Preserve existing names and relationships unless a deliberate retcon is approved.
- Mark new proposals as proposed until implemented and accepted.
- Keep local conflicts meaningful even when connected to the Seven Flames mystery.
- Do not make every conflict secretly caused by one villain.
- Do not make every monster a supernatural manifestation of the Covenant.
- Do not turn all factions into morally uniform groups.
- Do not promise branching outcomes unless campaign state records and later scenes acknowledge them.


## 13. The Last Emperor and the Age of Houses — post-campaign canon

**Status: OWNER-REQUESTED CANON DIRECTION / DESIGNED, NOT VERIFIED IMPLEMENTED.** This section establishes the intended endgame-to-generational transition. It does not make family gameplay available during the main campaign.

### The Emperor Who Ended the Empire

The main campaign's victory is not the founding of a permanent Dunmarrow dynasty on an imperial throne. The protagonist may, through the final campaign resolution, become the last Emperor of the old imperial order. This authority is deliberately transitional: it gives the protagonist the legal and political power to dismantle the institution that enabled the Covenant's betrayal, concentration of power and abuses of hereditary rule.

The protagonist's defining final act is the **Dissolution of the Empire**. The imperial crown, central court and claim of permanent supremacy are formally abolished. The Seven Houses and regional powers are not simply replaced by one new royal family. Instead, the protagonist establishes a successor settlement provisionally called the **Concord of Free Realms**: a compact of self-governing realms bound by mutual defence, open routes, famine relief, sanctuary, lawful arbitration and the surviving obligations of the Covenant of Seven Flames.

The Concord is not a utopia. It inherits border disputes, old debts, competing laws, ambitious nobles, displaced soldiers and communities that distrust every new promise. The protagonist's achievement is not that conflict disappears, but that no single imperial office can quietly rewrite the compact for everyone.

The protagonist is remembered in conflicting ways: liberator, oath-keeper, usurper, reluctant monarch, destroyer of the old order or founder of a fragile peace. Which account dominates depends on the campaign's recorded choices, companion fates, faction relations, treatment of occupied territories and the terms of dissolution.

### The long farewell

The main story does not abruptly hand control to a child at the final battle. After the imperial dissolution, an authored late-life epilogue follows the protagonist through a period of retirement from command, public duties, family or chosen household life, and the slow transfer of responsibilities. The protagonist can grow old, witness the first years of the Concord and see how the world interprets the new settlement.

The generational mode unlocks only when all of the following are true:
1. The main campaign's required final objective and ending resolution are complete.
2. The protagonist has become the last Emperor in the ending path.
3. The Dissolution of the Empire has been formally enacted and recorded.
4. The late-life epilogue has advanced the protagonist to a natural death from old age.
5. The death is recorded as a completed life event, not a battle casualty, scripted early death, or ordinary game-over.

Only after this sequence does the game open the **Chronicle of Houses** and transfer player control to the chosen eligible successor. No marriage, child-rearing, dynasty succession or playable family-management loop should be exposed during the main campaign. Main-campaign companions can have relationships and personal histories, but this is not the generational family simulation.

### The new political order

The Concord recognises local titles as offices of stewardship and responsibility rather than automatic ownership of people. Its founding charter distinguishes:
- **Household / landed gentry:** recognised family estate or civic holding, with obligations to its workers and neighbours.
- **Knight / Dame:** sworn service, land grant or recognised military-civic distinction; not automatically hereditary.
- **Baron / Baroness:** stewardship of a barony, its villages, roads, courts and levy obligations.
- **Viscount / Viscountess:** a delegated county office or oversight of several smaller holdings; title usage may vary by realm.
- **Count / Countess (Earl / Countess in some regions):** stewardship of a county, its settlements, revenues, justice and defence.
- **Marquess / Marchioness:** frontier march authority with exceptional defence and logistics duties.
- **Duke / Duchess:** stewardship of a duchy comprising multiple counties and major regional obligations.
- **Archduke / Archduchess:** a rare senior rank recognised by several realms, normally tied to a major historic region or compact responsibility.
- **Prince / Princess of a Free Realm:** a dynastic or elected-heir title used only by realms whose own constitutional law recognises it.
- **High Protector / First Warden:** exceptional non-imperial compact office, elected or appointed under strict limits; it is not a disguised Emperor.

These are a shared design ladder, not a claim that every culture uses identical titles. Preserve local terms and laws in each continent. A title must carry a defined duty, jurisdiction, source of legitimacy and accountability. Nobility is not simply a cosmetic colour or a universal combat-stat multiplier.

### The Chronicle of Houses

The post-campaign player follows a family or adopted house across generations. The original protagonist remains a historical founder and a remembered person, not a character who can be revived or replaced. The player's house can rise, fracture, lose land, regain trust, become extinct or survive through adoption and marriage alliances.

The central narrative question changes from “Can one person repair a broken realm?” to “Can a family preserve its obligations without becoming the kind of institution it once opposed?”

The Chronicle should remember:
- the protagonist's ending, final title and manner/age of death;
- the exact terms and signatories of imperial dissolution;
- companions, allies, rivals and family members who survived;
- lands granted, inherited, purchased, defended, lost or returned;
- treaties, wars, famines, reforms, betrayals and public works;
- each generation's marriages, children, guardians, education, traits, wounds, achievements and death;
- contested succession claims, inheritance settlements and local historical memory.

### Narrative safeguards

- Do not turn the protagonist's abolition of the Empire into a secret restoration of imperial rule under another name.
- Do not assume every realm accepts the Concord or the protagonist's settlement. Opposition and alternate political traditions should remain possible.
- Do not make every noble cruel or every commoner virtuous. Show competence, corruption, sacrifice and ambition at every rank.
- Bloodline must never be a measure of human worth. Hereditary qualities are narrative/gameplay variation, not racial purity or a eugenic optimisation ladder.
- Adoption, legitimisation, chosen heirs, capable daughters, younger children, collateral branches and non-biological succession must be viable story paths.
- A dynasty may lose its title or lands through lawful judgment, debt, rebellion, neglect or defeat; avoid arbitrary random destruction.
- Generational play is an optional post-campaign continuation, not a prerequisite for seeing the main story's ending.

## 14. Lore themes for the generational era

The post-campaign era should explore the tension between **inheritance and stewardship**:
- A baron inherits a keep but discovers the villages no longer trust the family.
- A count must decide whether to honour a famine compact that costs the county its winter surplus.
- A marquess must defend a frontier without treating border communities as expendable.
- A duke must arbitrate disputes among ambitious counts while maintaining the Concord.
- A younger child with no inherited title may earn office through service, scholarship, diplomacy or military leadership.
- An heir may reject a title, enter a religious or civic order, become a wandering adventurer, or challenge a parent's decisions.
- A marriage can unite claims but also create divided loyalties, rival heirs and diplomatic obligations.
- A beloved heir may be unsuited to rule; a disliked sibling may be the most capable steward.
- A family can preserve its name through adoption, a cadet branch or a chosen successor when direct descendants are absent.

Each generation should inherit both assets and unresolved consequences. The family chronicle is a record of obligations as much as prestige.

## 15. Full campaign story treatment — chapter, choice and companion canon

**Status:** Expanded narrative blueprint for future authoring. It is not a claim that these chapters, scenes, dialogue, cinematics or branches are already playable. The existing prologue/five-act/epilogue structure remains intact; this section expands it into a production-ready sequence while preserving established characters, places and constitutional canon.

### 15.1 Narrative rules and state variables

The story is about the cost of rebuilding trust, not a binary good/evil morality test. Every major choice should have at least two defensible motives, an immediate consequence, a delayed consequence and a human witness who remembers it. Avoid hidden morality points that silently decide the ending.

Track the following narrative state through named, explainable flags:
- **Company cohesion:** whether companions can disagree without abandoning one another.
- **Public trust:** how settlements judge the protagonist's conduct, especially food, justice, protection and civilian harm.
- **House legitimacy:** evidence supporting Dunmarrow's legal and political claims; distinct from popularity.
- **Covenant evidence:** which seals, records, witnesses and original clauses have been verified.
- **House commitments:** each of the Seven Houses' explicit promises, conditions and unresolved grievances.
- **Maelor's settlement channel:** whether communication and credible negotiation remain possible.
- **Companion arc outcomes:** personal quest resolutions and relationship changes, not a single universal approval score.
- **Concord ratification:** which realms and institutions consent to the post-imperial compact.

A branch may alter allies, resources, mission routes, casualties, testimony, council composition or the wording of a settlement. It must not contradict fixed canon without a clearly authored alternate-history explanation. The Last Emperor route is a specific true-ending branch, not the only emotionally valid ending.

### 15.2 Prologue — Smoke on the Wind

**Opening scene: The last evening at Kaer Dunmarrow.** Torren is introduced through ordinary duties: checking the gate, speaking to household staff, training with a companion and hearing Edmer argue with a messenger behind a closed door. The player sees affection and tension before the catastrophe. Edmer refuses to explain the sealed packet, but asks Torren to remember that a house is measured by whom it protects.

**Chapter P1 — The Ash Alarm.** The estate is attacked and set alight. The tutorial teaches movement, interaction, defence and retreat while the player navigates smoke, collapsing passages and separated household members. The player must choose a first rescue priority: the trapped household, the stable yard where the wounded gather, or the record room. The chosen route determines who can be saved immediately; a later rescue attempt may recover another group at a cost. Do not present the choice as a simple “save people versus save a book” moral trap.

**Chapter P2 — The Broken Road.** Survivors flee through the outer grounds. Bram Vosk holds a narrow passage; Sela Wrenfield identifies a safe route; Brother Wystan tends the injured. The player chooses whether to pursue a masked officer, protect the retreat, or send a witness ahead. Pursuit yields identifying evidence but risks casualties; protecting the retreat preserves people but loses the trail; sending the witness creates a later testimony thread.

**Chapter P3 — The First Account.** At a ruined waystation, the group compares memories. No one has the whole truth. A burned dispatch bears a Crownlands seal, but the order's author is unclear. The first journal entry distinguishes confirmed facts, testimony and speculation. End with Torren choosing whether to swear publicly to restore House Dunmarrow, discover the truth, or protect the survivors first. These are priorities, not exclusive quest locks.

### 15.3 Act I — Cold Hearth

**Chapter I.1 — Sallow Crossing.** The company reaches a winter settlement whose granary is threatened by armed raiders. Investigation reveals the raiders were paid to seize a ledger hidden among the grain tallies. The player can negotiate, ambush the raiders, evacuate families or defend the storehouse. Combat objectives and civilian safety depend on preparation.

**Chapter I.2 — The Winter Measure.** Food is insufficient for every claimant. The player hears from the reeve, an injured patrol, displaced families and a nearby miller. Distribution can prioritise need, preserve seed grain, honour an existing contract or divide stores under public oversight. No option feeds everyone; the aftermath records who bears the cost and whether the decision was transparent.

**Chapter I.3 — Names in the Ledger.** The recovered ledger links payments to intermediaries rather than directly to Maelor. A local official offers to destroy it in exchange for amnesty. The player can preserve the original, make a public copy, or use it to bargain for the release of detained villagers. Each route affects the reliability and reach of the evidence.

**Chapter I.4 — A Keep Worth Returning To.** The company returns to Kaer Sethorn, establishes a defensible refuge and meets local workers who distrust the Dunmarrow name. The player chooses the first visible repair: shelter, infirmary, stores or training yard. The choice changes dialogue and practical services, not the overall availability of core campaign systems. Act I closes when the company accepts that survival now requires a community.

### 15.4 Act II — Broken Seals

**Chapter II.1 — The Orrowen Hearing.** The company seeks a hearing from an Orrowen representative. The public Covenant is invoked by both reformers and traditionalists. The player must distinguish the ancient compact from the later secret organisation that appropriated its name. Evidence is presented in a hearing where procedure, witnesses and local grievances matter as much as rhetoric.

**Chapter II.2 — The Witness's Price.** A witness recants after threats against their family. The player can expose the coercion, relocate the family, accept a narrower statement or proceed without the witness. A forced public confession is not treated as reliable proof. Sela's arc is foregrounded: she knows how survival distorts testimony and challenges Torren to protect the witness without owning their story.

**Chapter II.3 — The Road of Three Banners.** During a diplomatic escort, three armed groups claim lawful authority over the same road. The mission can be resolved by identifying forged orders, securing a local arbitration or defeating the group that attacks civilians. A victory by force can clear the road but undermine the hearing if the company ignores local law.

**Chapter II.4 — The First Flame.** The company secures a Covenant seal or a verified record associated with it. The object does not cast a convenient spell or reveal all truth. It authenticates a specific oath and points toward the next custodian. The act ends with the discovery that each House preserved a different part of the compact and has reasons to distrust the others.

### 15.5 Act III — The River Campaign

**Chapter III.1 — A River Divided.** A bridge and its surrounding villages are contested. The player surveys ferries, flood levels, supply stores and civilian routes before choosing an approach. The campaign map should communicate time and supply consequences before commitment.

**Chapter III.2 — The False Banner.** An apparent enemy convoy is carrying refugees under a borrowed military banner. The player can halt and inspect it, escort it, divert it through a dangerous route or attack after a mistaken identification. The scene tests intelligence and restraint; the consequences must be legible rather than sprung as an arbitrary punishment.

**Chapter III.3 — The Bridge at Redwater.** A multi-stage operation combines reconnaissance, control of the riverbank and a tactical battle with an objective beyond eliminating every enemy. Possible objectives include holding the crossing until civilians pass, disabling siege equipment or capturing the quartermaster alive. The chosen objective affects later supply and testimony.

**Chapter III.4 — The Price of Passage.** A local commander offers safe passage in return for a levy on the villages. The player can refuse, negotiate a time-limited toll, accept under public accounting or expose the commander's private profiteering. The act closes with a message that the Quillon archive contains a version of the Codicil that contradicts Edmer's account.

### 15.6 Act IV — Winter of Knives

**Chapter IV.1 — The Quillon Archive.** The company enters the archive through invitation, legal petition, covert access or a negotiated exchange. Each route has a distinct cost. The archive is not a simple dungeon full of interchangeable enemies: its librarians, custodians and records embody competing interpretations of lawful preservation.

**Chapter IV.2 — The Missing Clause.** The Codicil reveals that Edmer concealed one clause to protect a person or community from retaliation. The hidden clause must be authored as a concrete obligation, not a vague twist. Edmer's choice saved someone in the short term but deprived the Houses of information needed to hold him accountable. Torren must decide whether to publish the full record, redact identifying details or submit it first to independent custodians.

**Chapter IV.3 — The Company Divided.** Companions disagree about the next step. Bram favours a defensible military position; Sela insists that witnesses must retain control of their testimony; Wystan argues that mercy without restitution is only another form of silence. The player can mediate, choose a temporary plan or allow a companion to lead a mission according to their expertise. Disagreement should not trigger arbitrary betrayal.

**Chapter IV.4 — The Regent's Offer.** Maelor sends a credible offer: a limited amnesty, food and security guarantees, and a lawful hearing, in exchange for surrendering the Codicil and abandoning the claim to expose his role. The offer can save lives but risks burying the public compact again. The player can accept talks, reject them publicly, demand neutral witnesses or use the meeting to learn what Maelor fears most. Maelor's argument—that sudden disclosure may cause famine and civil war—must be heard in its strongest form before the player judges his methods.

### 15.7 Act V — The Kindling

**Chapter V.1 — Seven Tables.** Representatives of the Seven Houses gather, but not all arrive with equal authority or willingness. The Crownlands monarchy remains a separate royal institution. House Ysmark's seat must not be invented or assigned to Brinegate; Brinegate is the Tidebound Assembly's seat. The player builds an agenda around verified clauses, food obligations, conscription limits, sanctuary and the common towns' right of appeal.

**Chapter V.2 — The City Under Strain.** Aurelmont faces a multi-front crisis: armed loyalists, frightened residents, disrupted food routes and opportunists using the secret Covenant faction's name. The player chooses whether to prioritise evacuation, secure the granaries, protect the hearing chamber or disable the command network. A tactical victory that harms civilians can damage the legitimacy of the settlement.

**Chapter V.3 — The Last Audience.** Torren meets Maelor. The confrontation is personal but also constitutional. Maelor admits what he ordered and explains what he believed he prevented. He may stand trial, accept exile, negotiate a lawful surrender or die in battle depending on prior state and the player's actions. No branch should quietly absolve him of responsibility. If he survives, his testimony is evidence, not automatic forgiveness.

**Chapter V.4 — The Council of Seven.** The Houses and recognised communities decide whether the Hidden Codicil is authentic and how its obligations should be restored. The Crownlands' single-crown authority transfers to a Council of the Seven Houses only after the required lawful ratifications. The Council's powers, limits, representation and accountability are stated in the settlement. A failed ratification produces a contested or fractured ending rather than an instant global government.

**Chapter V.5 — The Shape of Peace.** The player chooses the political settlement available from accumulated evidence and commitments:
- **Council settlement:** the Council governs the Crownlands under a written charter, with public oversight and defined limits.
- **Plural compact:** willing realms enter a looser agreement while others retain distance or neutrality.
- **Restoration settlement:** a lawful Crownlands monarchy survives under binding Covenant reforms; this is a valid non-true ending if supported by the campaign state.
- **Fractured settlement:** the crisis ends militarily or through a narrow pact, but unresolved Houses and regions remain in dispute.

Only the designated true-ending route proceeds to the temporary Last Emperor office. It requires the Council of Seven to be constituted, the necessary house/realm ratifications, verified Codicil evidence and a settlement that authorises the dormant imperial charter solely to complete dissolution. The protagonist is not crowned through conquest or personal ambition.

### 15.8 True ending — The Last Ember

**The Investiture.** The Council invests Torren as Last Emperor under the dormant constitutional office. The ceremony is deliberately restrained: no triumphant world-conquest coronation. The mandate is narrow, time-limited and publicly recorded. The protagonist accepts it as a burden to end the old imperial claim, not as a prize.

**The Dissolution Charter.** The player confirms the clauses that dissolve the imperial office, central claim and remaining imperial institutions. Each signatory realm retains its own laws. Realms that refuse the Concord remain outside it; their choice is recorded rather than overwritten. The charter prevents ordinary inheritance, purchase or revival of the Emperor title.

**The Concord of Free Realms.** The successor compact is founded through willing ratification. It establishes mutual aid, dispute arbitration, protection of travel and sanctuary, famine obligations and limits on coercive levies. It does not create a secret empire under another name. The player sees which promises were accepted, qualified or refused.

**The Late Years.** A sequence of authored vignettes advances through the protagonist's later life. Torren visits Kaer Sethorn, receives former companions, hears disputes among signatories and sees both successes and failures of the Concord. The player can choose how to spend the remaining years: public mediation, rebuilding, private life, mentoring successors or documenting the truth. These scenes should show age through changed movement, voice and relationships, not merely a number.

**Natural Death and Founder Chronicle.** Only after the late-life epilogue is complete and the protagonist dies naturally of old age is the Founder Chronicle sealed. The scene should be quiet and personal. The game records the exact ending, dissolution charter, Concord signatories, companions, surviving relationships, deeds, unresolved obligations and cause of death. This event unlocks Chronicle of Houses. Battle death, sacrifice, defeat, retirement, skipped epilogue or any other ending does not satisfy the gate.

### 15.9 Companion arcs and resolution beats

These arcs are intended to be woven into the chapters above. Their outcomes depend on choices and personal quests; no companion is guaranteed to agree with the protagonist.

- **Bram Vosk — The Weight of the Shield.** Begins believing that safety comes from obedience and a strong defensive line. His personal quest follows a former unit whose orders caused civilian deaths. The player can help him testify, seek restitution or suppress the record to preserve military cohesion. Resolution: Bram learns that loyalty can include refusing an unlawful order. Depending on choices, he may remain as commander, become a trainer at Kaer Sethorn, leave to make amends or refuse a political office.
- **Sela Wrenfield — The Witness Who Chooses.** Begins distrustful of official accounts and protective of informants. Her quest follows a network of displaced families who carry pieces of the truth. The player must protect their agency rather than use them as evidence. Resolution: Sela may become an independent investigator, a public recorder for the Council, a scout captain or leave the company if Torren repeatedly compromises witnesses.
- **Brother Wystan — Mercy and the Measure of Justice.** Begins faithful but uncertain that powerful people speak for the Covenant. His quest concerns a sanctuary whose protection has been abused by armed fugitives and denied to innocent families. The player balances mercy, safety and restitution. Resolution: Wystan may establish a sanctuary, serve as an independent mediator, return to a religious community or step away from public life.
- **Edmer Dunmarrow — The Inheritance of Silence.** Edmer appears through letters, testimony and memory rather than as a companion who can be restored. His record gradually reveals courage and culpable concealment. The final judgment of Edmer should remain contested among survivors; Torren may honour his sacrifice without declaring every decision correct.
- **Maelor Brennhallow — The Necessary Tyrant's Argument.** Maelor's arc is not redemption by a single speech. His choices, orders, victims and willingness to accept lawful judgment determine whether he is captured, tried, exiled, killed or remains an unrepentant fugitive. His fear of collapse is credible; the harm he caused remains real.
- **Captain Ilyra Venn and pilot Maren Vey — The Beacon and the Tide.** Their Thalassar/Brinegate Coast storyline should remain regionally grounded. Ilyra's leadership and Maren's knowledge of routes expose how maritime communities interpret the public Covenant and the secret faction's appropriation of its name. Their choices can earn the company passage, witnesses or a maritime alliance; they do not make Brinegate House Ysmark's seat.

### 15.10 Ending matrix and continuity safeguards

The campaign should support endings that feel complete even when they do not unlock the generational mode:
1. **The Restored Crown:** a reformed Crownlands monarchy survives under enforceable Covenant limits. The protagonist may secure justice for Dunmarrow but does not become Last Emperor.
2. **The Council of Houses:** the Crownlands transfers authority to a functioning Council, but the dormant imperial office is not reconstituted or the required dissolution terms are not completed.
3. **The Concord of Free Realms — True Ending:** Council and realm ratifications authorise the Last Emperor's temporary mandate; the Empire is formally abolished; the Concord is founded; late-life epilogue concludes; natural old-age death seals the Founder Chronicle and unlocks Chronicle of Houses.
4. **The Fractured Peace:** a military or negotiated end stops immediate conflict but leaves Houses or regions outside a stable settlement. The protagonist's fate and companions' outcomes reflect the route.
5. **The Ashen Victory:** the protagonist defeats Maelor but uses coercive methods that undermine public trust and the Covenant's obligations. Victory is real but politically brittle.

No ending should be labelled “good” solely because more territory is controlled or more companions survive. The true ending is defined by constitutional completion and the strict unlock sequence, not by a perfect approval score. The post-campaign family simulation begins only after natural old-age death in that true-ending route.

### 15.11 Production and implementation guidance

For each chapter, author a scene packet containing: entry conditions; location and cast; opening dialogue intent; objectives; optional objectives; encounter and non-combat routes; choice IDs; state changes; failure/recovery paths; companion reactions; journal updates; exit conditions; and continuity references. Every major choice needs a later callback or a recorded reason why it did not recur.

Use stable IDs for chapters, quests, decisions, flags and endings. Keep prose/dialogue separate from logic. Avoid branching every scene into an unmaintainable tree: prefer a shared chapter spine with state-dependent scenes, alternate mission approaches and variable aftermath. Preserve offline operation and existing save compatibility. Do not claim any story chapter is playable until implemented and tested in Godot.
