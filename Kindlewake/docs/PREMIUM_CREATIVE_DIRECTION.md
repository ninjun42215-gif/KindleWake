# KindleWake — Premium Narrative, World & Player Experience Direction

**Status:** Production direction proposal for the next content/quality milestone  
**Branch checkpoint:** `6379826a992e67a2abd92a9a795d97cb1022e4ca`  
**Project identity:** `Kindlewake: The Sundered Vow`  
**Principle:** Aim for the coherence, craft, presentation and emotional impact associated with a premium, large-budget RPG. This is a quality target—not a claim about actual budget, team size or production status.

---

## 1. Creative director's mandate

KindleWake should feel like a world that continues to exist when the player is not looking. The player should care about the people in their company, recognise places by their history and visual identity, and feel that every major decision changes the political and personal landscape.

The game must not become a checklist of disconnected systems. Tactical combat, recruitment, bonds, rune crafting, exploration, town services, fortress growth and the main story must reinforce one another.

### Five non-negotiable pillars

1. **Human stakes before lore volume.** Every major plot beat is grounded in a character's fear, loyalty, ambition, grief or contradiction. Lore is discovered through people and consequences, not exposition dumps.
2. **A memorable company.** Recruits have a voice, visual identity, combat niche, relationships, personal quest and a reason to remain with the company. The player should be able to name a favourite and explain why.
3. **A changing home.** Kaer Sethorn visibly and socially changes. Rebuilding unlocks useful services, new NPC routines, ambient scenes and story conversations—not just stat bonuses.
4. **War has a human cost.** Battles change control, supplies, morale, relationships, recruitment and later scenes. Victory is not always morally clean; defeat creates a recovery story rather than a dead end.
5. **Respect the player's time.** Long sessions are supported by layered optional content and satisfying stopping points. No artificial energy timers, punitive daily streaks, manipulative FOMO or grind walls.

## 2. Narrative promise and revised dramatic spine

### The promise

A condemned heir inherits the fortress that was meant to bury their family. To learn why their father died branded a traitor, they must rebuild a broken company, earn the trust of six rival houses and decide whether a kingdom can be held together by a crown, a covenant or the people who live beneath them.

### Preserve established canon

The following are established foundations and should not be casually renamed or contradicted:

- Eldrimar and the Covenant of the Seven Flames.
- House Dunmarrow as keeper of the Covenant; Kaer Sethorn as the ruined home.
- Lord Edmer Dunmarrow's execution for treason.
- King Osric IV's death and the Hidden Codicil.
- Regent Maelor Brennhallow's belief that a strong crown is the only barrier against civil war.
- The six surviving great houses, their regional identities and their competing ideas of order.
- Torren / the player-created heir, Bram, Sela and Brother Wystan as the opening company.

### The emotional engine

The central question is not simply “Who killed the king?” It is:

**When the truth threatens to destroy the peace, do you owe the realm the truth—or the lives that truth may cost?**

Maelor must remain a credible antagonist. He should make defensible arguments, protect ordinary people in some situations and commit unforgivable acts in others. The player's opposition should be ideological and personal, not a simple battle between good and evil.

### A stronger reveal architecture

The Hidden Codicil is not a magical solution or a convenient final document. It is evidence of a political bargain that all seven houses helped create and later betrayed in different ways. Each house possesses one fragment of the historical record: a witness, ledger, oath, military order, sealed testimony or relic. The player reconstructs the truth by earning trust and investigating contradictions.

The Seven Flames should carry both symbolic and practical meaning: each flame represents a founding obligation (protection, justice, provision, memory, restraint, sanctuary and consent). The Covenant failed because the houses selectively honoured the obligations that benefited them. The codicil exposes this failure and proposes a council—but does not guarantee that a council will be just.

**Guardrail:** this is a proposed thematic expansion. Do not implement new magical cosmology, seven collectible keys or a prophecy unless it strengthens character agency and fits the existing low-magic tone.

## 3. Campaign structure: a character-led prestige drama

Each act should have a distinct dramatic question, visual identity, tactical signature, companion focus and irreversible decision. Main missions should alternate pressure with quieter recovery scenes.

| Chapter | Dramatic question | Player experience | Character focus | Lasting consequence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Prologue — Smoke on the Wind | What is worth saving when home is burning? | Escape, tutorial battle, first refuge | Player heir, Bram, Sela, Wystan | Who is saved, what evidence survives |
| I — Cold Hearth | Can a ruined house become a home? | Rebuild Sethorn, first recruits, local crisis | Founding company and villagers | First policy for refugees, taxes and protection |
| II — Broken Seals | Can trust be earned from people who fear your name? | Orrowen and Ysmark investigations, first strategic battle | Sela, Joss, Verra and regional leaders | A house becomes ally, rival or wary neutral |
| III — The River Campaign | What does victory cost the people who follow you? | Vessmoor campaign, siege, supply decisions | Isaure, Bram, Edda and field commanders | Casualties, displaced families, command reputation |
| IV — Winter of Knives | How much truth can a company survive? | Quillon and Tarnholt revelations, infiltration, divided loyalties | Wystan, Odalys, Orlan, Kestrel | A trusted relationship may fracture or deepen |
| V — The Kindling | What should replace a broken order? | Aurelmont campaign, multi-front climax, final negotiation | Full company and surviving house leaders | Constitutional future of Eldrimar |
| Epilogue — The Chronicle | What did your company become? | Personal outcomes, rebuilt places, memorials | Every recruited character | Epilogue scenes respond to bonds, choices and losses |

### Chapter rhythm

A chapter is a sequence of authored beats, not just a list of battles:

1. **Cold open:** a striking image or urgent human problem.
2. **Orientation:** a concise objective and clear stakes.
3. **Investigation / travel:** discover a contradiction, recruit lead or optional local story.
4. **Preparation:** meaningful party, gear, rune and formation decisions.
5. **Set-piece:** tactical encounter with a clear battlefield identity and at least one objective twist.
6. **Aftermath:** casualty / resource / political consequences are explained in plain language.
7. **Character scene:** a recruit reacts, argues, confesses or asks for help.
8. **Forward hook:** one strong unanswered question leads into the next chapter.

Do not force all eight beats into every short mission. Use them as a pacing grammar across the chapter.

## 4. Make the company unforgettable

The 24-character Wave-1 roster is the foundation. Quality comes from depth and interplay before raw roster size.

### Character production standard

Every recruit intended to be a named companion must eventually have:

- A concise public identity and a private contradiction.
- A personal want, a fear and a line they will not cross.
- A recognisable speaking rhythm; avoid giving everyone the same fantasy-poetic voice.
- At least two meaningful relationship links to other recruits.
- A combat identity that creates a real party-building decision.
- A recruitment scene that demonstrates their character under pressure.
- A personal quest with a choice, not just a fetch objective.
- A bond progression with at least three authored beats: first trust, tested trust and earned commitment.
- A visual silhouette, portrait and signature prop or costume detail.
- A campaign outcome that reflects player decisions without requiring perfect loyalty.

### Relationship writing rules

- Characters should disagree with each other for understandable reasons.
- Banter must reflect current events, location, party composition and relationship state.
- A companion should sometimes be right when the player disagrees.
- High loyalty is not mind control; a loyal companion can still challenge a harmful order.
- Low loyalty should create readable warning signs and opportunities to repair trust before severe consequences.
- Do not kill or permanently remove a companion merely to manufacture shock. If a loss occurs, establish foreshadowing, agency and meaningful aftermath.

### Opening company direction

- **Bram:** the veteran who knows the truth of the escape but has mistaken silence for protection. His arc is accountability, not merely loyalty.
- **Sela:** the practical outsider who sees how noble conflict harms common people. Her arc tests whether she can influence the house without becoming what she hates.
- **Wystan:** a gentle priest whose faith is not simply lost or restored; he must decide what faith obliges him to do when institutions fail.
- **The player heir:** define their values through consequential choices and companion reactions. The custom appearance and origin must be respected; do not force a fixed personality or unskippable romance.

## 5. Choices with visible consequences

Every major choice should be tracked as a named, testable campaign flag or relationship change. A choice should not be presented as consequential if it only changes one line of dialogue.

Use a consequence ledger for each authored decision:

| Field | Required design |
|---|---|
| Immediate outcome | What changes in the current scene or mission? |
| Human outcome | Who benefits, who is harmed, who remembers? |
| Strategic outcome | What changes in supplies, control, recruitment or access? |
| Relationship outcome | Which characters approve, object or change their bond? |
| Delayed callback | Where and when does the player see the result again? |
| Recovery path | Can a damaged relationship or political position be repaired? |
| Ending relevance | Which ending conditions are affected, if any? |

### Example choice pattern

At a famine-struck crossing, the player can requisition grain for the army, distribute it to the settlement, or negotiate a levy with the local house.

- Army-first improves immediate supply and may reduce battle difficulty, but creates resentment and a later refugee problem.
- Settlement-first earns trust and recruits but forces a harder tactical supply challenge.
- Negotiation costs time or gold but can preserve both, provided the player has earned the right contacts.

All three paths should be viable. Avoid a hidden “correct” choice that is only revealed by a later punishment.

## 6. Session design and long-play engagement

The goal is a compelling multi-hour session because the player is curious and invested—not because the game refuses to let them stop.

### The satisfying 30–60 minute loop

- A visible objective the player can understand immediately.
- One meaningful tactical or exploration challenge.
- One character, recruitment or world discovery.
- A tangible reward that changes a build or unlocks a choice.
- A clear next lead and a safe save point.

### The satisfying 2–4 hour session

A longer run should naturally connect a chapter mission, a new location, a recruit or bond scene, a fortress upgrade and a follow-up battle. The player should be able to stop after any major beat without losing progress.

### Engagement systems to prioritise

1. **Quest journal:** main objective, optional leads, known clues, character requests and completed consequences; every entry has a clear next step.
2. **World reactivity:** NPCs, shop stock, ambient dialogue, banners, patrols and settlement conditions respond to chapter and choices.
3. **Company moments:** camp conversations, meals, training rivalries, repairs, memorials and quiet character scenes between battles.
4. **Battle variety:** rotate objective types, map geometry, weather, reinforcements, hazards and enemy command behaviours. Do not inflate enemy HP as the main difficulty tool.
5. **Build discovery:** rune combinations, class paths, equipment trade-offs and platoon synergies should produce understandable, testable strategies.
6. **Reward clarity:** previews show what an item, recruit, facility or rune changes before the player commits.
7. **Catch-up and recap:** returning players get a short “Previously in your Chronicle” summary and a clear next objective.
8. **Respectful saves:** autosave after major outcomes; communicate save status; never punish app suspension or interrupted mobile sessions.

### Avoid

- Daily login streaks, energy systems, loot boxes and paid power.
- Repetitive random encounters used to pad campaign length.
- Excessive dialogue before the player can act.
- Long unskippable tutorials.
- Missable critical recruits without warning or an alternate route.
- Fake choice, opaque fusion odds or hidden irreversible costs.
- Menus that require repeated backtracking to compare gear, runes or party members.

## 7. Worldbuilding through place

Every settlement, dungeon and region needs a distinct reason to exist beyond its name.

A location brief must define:

- Its economy and what people make, grow, trade or lack.
- Its architecture, materials, rooflines, colours and weather.
- Its social hierarchy and who has power in public versus private.
- One local custom, one rumour and one unresolved grievance.
- One environmental storytelling detail that rewards observation.
- A service or gameplay function that is useful to the player.
- A quest that reveals something about the wider conflict.
- A return-state change after the player's actions.

### Regional identity

- **Sethmarch:** damp stone, repaired timber, river mud, old toll roads and a frontier culture that distrusts distant authority.
- **Orrowen:** layered canopy, green-black timber, carved boundary stones, ranger trails and disputes over who owns the forest.
- **Vessmoor:** open sky, wind-bent grass, red-gold cloth, horse infrastructure and honour used as both virtue and political weapon.
- **Ysmark Coast:** salt-stained masonry, rope, ship timber, counting houses and a culture that prices information.
- **Quillon Heights:** steep streets, slate roofs, observatories, archives and knowledge guarded as property.
- **Tarnholt:** pale stone, hospices, ash gardens, pilgrim roads and the tension between compassion and institutional control.
- **Brennhallow:** iron, black stone, disciplined geometry, siege engineering and a population that values predictable order.

Do not build all regions from recoloured copies of one map. Each needs a different traversal silhouette, landmark language and soundscape.

## 8. Art and audio quality bar

### Art direction

Target a coherent, authored pixel-art world with painterly composition and restrained effects. Every asset should appear to belong to the same game, even when sourced from different artists or packs.

**Required production passes:**

1. **Asset audit:** record source, licence, permitted edits, attribution, resolution, palette and intended use. Unknown or non-commercial assets stay out of distributable builds.
2. **Style bible:** lock palette ramps, outline weight, light direction, material rendering, perspective, scale and animation timing.
3. **Silhouette pass:** ensure each class, enemy family, building and faction is recognisable at phone scale.
4. **Animation pass:** consistent pivots and foot placement; anticipation, impact and recovery; readable hit reactions; avoid motion that obscures tactical information.
5. **Environment pass:** tile transitions, edges, height readability, props, clutter control, lighting and foreground/background separation.
6. **UI pass:** consistent parchment/metal materials, hierarchy, icon language, focus states, safe areas, contrast and scalable text.
7. **Polish pass:** particles, weather, subtle environmental motion, screen transitions and contextual sound—only where they improve feedback.

### Asset priority order

| Priority | Asset family | Quality objective |
|---|---|---|
| P0 | Player heir and four starting companions | Distinct portraits, combat silhouettes, signature equipment and readable animations |
| P0 | Core UI kit | Consistent panels, buttons, tabs, icons, typography and touch states |
| P0 | Sethorn, Sallow Crossing and first battle maps | Strong composition, clear routes, landmarks and interactable props |
| P1 | Six house heraldry sets | Banners, shields, uniforms, seals and colour identity |
| P1 | Enemy families and bosses | Distinct silhouettes, attack tells, hit/death reactions and boss staging |
| P1 | Town interiors and facilities | Unique functional spaces, NPC staging and environmental storytelling |
| P2 | Additional companion portraits and personal quest scenes | Expression range and emotional continuity |
| P2 | Regional ambience, music and battle stingers | Motifs that evolve with story and location |

### Audio direction

Build a recognisable musical identity around a small number of original motifs: the Dunmarrow theme, the Covenant motif, the company theme and the regency theme. Re-orchestrate them by region, relationship state and story outcome. Add restrained interface sounds, distinct tactical hit cues, environmental ambience and silence where a scene needs emotional weight.

No copyrighted soundtrack imitation or unlicensed audio. Keep mobile audio mix clear at low volume and support mute/volume settings.

### Provenance gate

The existing asset documentation identifies packs with unresolved licensing. Those assets must not be treated as production-cleared until authoritative terms and attribution are recorded. Replace uncertain assets with original commissioned work or assets with compatible, documented licences. Never remove provenance to make a build pass.

## 9. Mobile-first UX quality bar

The mobile interface is the primary product, not a shrunken desktop interface.

- Every primary action must be reachable with one thumb where practical.
- Touch targets should be at least 48 dp, with generous spacing and clear pressed/disabled/focused states.
- Important text must remain readable on small landscape screens and must respect system safe areas.
- Critical actions require clear confirmation and a visible cost/consequence preview.
- Gear and rune comparison should be possible without losing the selected character or scroll position.
- The player must be able to inspect enemy intent, terrain, status, turn order and attack outcomes before committing.
- Loading, saving, errors and rewards must have explicit feedback.
- Controller, keyboard and touch paths should share the same rules and remain testable.
- Add adjustable text size, colour-safe status indicators, reduced motion, reduced flashes and audio controls.
- Avoid tiny modal buttons, stacked confirmation dialogs and hidden swipe-only interactions.

### UX acceptance test

A new player should be able to start a chronicle, understand the immediate objective, move, interact, enter the first battle, inspect an enemy, perform an action, claim the outcome and save without external instructions. A returning player should be able to continue and understand what happened in under a minute.

## 10. Quality assurance: no illusion of polish

A premium presentation requires reliable foundations.

### Automated gates

- JSON/schema validation for every data-driven character, recruit, quest, location, battle, item and rune.
- Dialogue reference validation: no missing speaker, portrait, choice target, flag or localisation key.
- Campaign graph validation: no softlocks, unreachable required missions or impossible recruitment prerequisites.
- Save migration tests for every supported previous schema.
- Battle simulation and balance reports across classes, levels, enemy archetypes and difficulty bands.
- UI smoke tests at compact landscape, wide landscape and portrait sizes.
- Asset manifest checks for missing files, duplicate IDs, unlicensed files and unreasonable texture dimensions.
- APK checks for signature, architecture, size and absence of INTERNET permission.
- Exact-head CI before any merge.

### Human QA

- First-time player usability session.
- Chapter pacing and narrative continuity review.
- Accessibility review.
- Physical Android device matrix: small screen, notch, lower memory, older GPU and current device.
- Battery, thermal, load-time and memory profiling.
- Full save/restore, app suspend/resume and interrupted-session tests.
- Playtest notes tagged by severity and reproducible steps.

CI passing is not the same as device QA. A polished screenshot is not proof that an interaction works.

## 11. Production roadmap

Work in complete, playable vertical slices. Do not add a dozen disconnected features at once.

### Phase A — Narrative foundation and opening-hour rewrite
- Lock the narrative bible, chapter beats, faction motivations and companion arcs.
- Audit all existing dialogue, quest flags and current campaign flow against canon.
- Build the full opening sequence as a playable vertical slice: title → character creation → escape → tutorial battle → first refuge → first recruit/bond scene → Sethorn arrival.
- Add a journal/chronicle recap and clear objective tracking.
- Validate that choices in the opening have persistent, testable callbacks.

**Exit criteria:** a new player can finish the opening hour with a clear emotional connection, one meaningful decision, a memorable battle and a strong reason to continue.

### Phase B — Company and fortress reactivity
- Personal quests for the starting company and first recruit wave.
- Bond scenes, companion banter and relationship callbacks.
- Visible Sethorn restoration stages and NPC routines.
- Recruitment and facility unlocks integrated with the main chapter arc.

**Exit criteria:** the player can describe how the company and home changed because of their actions.

### Phase C — Regional world and encounter craft
- Distinct regional map kits, towns, dungeons, local conflicts and boss mechanics.
- Authored side quests with meaningful outcomes.
- Reduce repetitive random encounters; add authored patrols, discoveries and tactical variations.

**Exit criteria:** each region is recognisable without relying on its nameplate and has at least one memorable story encounter.

### Phase D — Art, animation and audio production
- Replace unresolved placeholder/source-pack assets with cleared or original assets.
- Complete key character portraits and class silhouettes.
- Add region-specific environment sets, effects, soundscapes and music motifs.
- Profile and optimise all assets for Android.

**Exit criteria:** visual and audio language is coherent from title screen through battle and aftermath, without sacrificing frame rate or readability.

### Phase E — Full campaign and release readiness
- Complete the five-act story and endings.
- Validate every major branch, companion state and save migration.
- Run accessibility, device, performance and narrative QA.
- Create release notes, credits, asset licences and player-facing help.

**Exit criteria:** no known blocker, no unlicensed release asset, no critical softlock, and a complete tested path from new game to epilogue.

## 12. Immediate implementation order

The next implementation should follow this order:

1. Audit current campaign scripts/data and identify the actual playable opening sequence; do not assume GDD design notes are implemented.
2. Map every existing story flag, recruit gate and current location to the narrative chapter structure.
3. Create a data-driven dialogue/quest format only after confirming the current runtime can load, display, save and test it.
4. Implement one complete opening quest chain with at least one consequential choice and a delayed callback.
5. Add a compact journal/recap only when it can read the same canonical quest state as gameplay.
6. Commission or create original P0 art assets in the locked style; preserve all current provenance.
7. Run exact-head strict tests and APK build; then do physical-device QA.

Do not bump the app version or save schema merely for this direction document. Do not label planned content as implemented. Keep each code/content milestone small enough to review and test.

## 13. Creative director's final test

Before accepting any new story, feature, asset or UI change, ask:

- Does it make the world more believable?
- Does it make a character more memorable?
- Does it give the player a meaningful decision or a satisfying new capability?
- Does it make the next session more inviting without manipulating the player?
- Does it improve clarity, responsiveness or emotional impact on a real phone?
- Is it original, legally usable, performant and tested?
- Does it connect to an existing system rather than adding isolated complexity?

If the answer is no, revise it or leave it out.
