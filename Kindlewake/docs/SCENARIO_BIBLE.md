# KindleWake — Scenario and Narrative Production Bible
## The Sundered Vow

**Purpose:** turn the established lore into authored, production-ready story scenarios with clear dramatic goals, player agency, tactical purpose and persistent consequences.  
**Status:** narrative design direction. These scenarios are not claims that the corresponding scenes, quests or branches are playable.  
**Canon authority:** the live character, continent, faction, territory, quest and enemy data; `LORE_BIBLE.md`; the existing `GDD.md`; and `PREMIUM_PRODUCTION_BIBLE.md`. If a discrepancy exists, flag it for deliberate resolution—do not silently retcon it.

---

## 1. Narrative contract

### The story in one sentence
A dispossessed heir gathers people abandoned by the realm, uncovers the obligations hidden beneath its founding Covenant, and must decide whether truth can become a more humane order—or merely another instrument of rule.

### What the player should feel
1. **Precarity:** the first victories are small, expensive and personal.
2. **Belonging:** Kaer Sethorn becomes a home because people repair it, argue in it and return to it.
3. **Complicity:** every faction has a reason for its choices; the player cannot remain morally untouched.
4. **Discovery:** each revelation reinterprets evidence already encountered.
5. **Responsibility:** power expands faster than certainty; the player must listen, negotiate and accept limits.
6. **Aftermath:** the ending is measured not only by who wins, but by who can safely live with the settlement.

### Five non-negotiable writing rules
- **No omniscient protagonist.** The player only knows what they have witnessed, been told or reasonably inferred.
- **No disposable civilians.** Refugees, labourers, tenants, sailors and soldiers have names, needs and conflicting views where the scene warrants it.
- **No villain-only institutions.** Institutions can protect people and still commit injustice; reformers can cause harm; opponents can be right about a danger.
- **No consequence-free virtue.** Mercy, honesty, restraint and generosity may be right, but they can still cost time, resources, allies or political leverage.
- **No false choice.** Choices must change a person’s circumstances, a relationship, a resource, a future option or the player’s understanding. Cosmetic dialogue variants must be labelled as such in implementation notes.

## 2. Mystery architecture: reveal the Covenant in layers

The Hidden Codicil should not be a single document that explains the entire plot. Treat it as a chain of testimony, physical evidence, contradictory records and living obligations.

| Layer | Evidence | Initial interpretation | Later reinterpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| The fire | A damaged household record and inconsistent witness accounts | The estate was destroyed to erase Dunmarrow evidence | Someone altered the record before the fire; the fire concealed more than one act |
| The winter stores | A ledger and paid raider testimony | Bandits attacked a vulnerable settlement | The raiders were paid to seize a ledger, while the shortage was already being manipulated |
| The public hearing | Conflicting copies of a Covenant clause | A local lord is breaking an old custom | Different Houses preserved different edits, each claiming its version prevented a greater harm |
| The river crossing | Refugees travelling under an enemy banner | A hostile supply movement | The banner is cover for civilians fleeing a forced relocation or unlawful levy |
| The Quillon archive | Edmer’s private notes and a missing clause | Edmer was a pure whistleblower | Edmer concealed one clause to protect someone; his courage and compromise coexist |
| Aurelmont | The complete Codicil and Maelor’s proposed settlement | The truth will settle the succession | The truth exposes obligations, but cannot itself feed, shelter or reconcile the realm |

**Fair-play rule:** plant at least two independent clues before a major reveal. One may be ambiguous; the other should become clear in hindsight. Never solve a mystery through a newly introduced witness who could have appeared earlier but conveniently did not.

## 3. Main campaign scenario spine

The intended structure remains a prologue, five acts and an epilogue. The sequence below sharpens the dramatic engine without asserting that every chapter is already implemented.

### Prologue — Smoke on the Wind
**Dramatic question:** What can be saved when a home is deliberately made unsafe?

**Opening image:** the Dunmarrow estate at night. Smoke obscures the family crest; the bell does not ring in its usual pattern. The player-character is not given a full explanation. They hear a familiar voice calling from one direction and see the household record exposed to the flames in another.

**Playable objectives:**
- Reach the inner yard while the fire and collapsing passages change the safe route.
- Decide whether to rescue a trapped household member, recover the damaged record, or split the few available helpers and risk both.
- Escape with Bram and the surviving witnesses before the gate is sealed.

**Choice and cost:** the rescue decision determines who survives or what evidence remains legible. Do not make the choice a disguised “good/evil” test. A rescued person may later disagree with the protagonist; a recovered record may be incomplete or misleading.

**Reversal:** the fire was not simply an attempt to kill the heir. The household record had been altered before the blaze. Someone wanted a particular version of events to survive.

**Quiet aftermath:** the survivors reach Kaer Sethorn. No triumphant speech. The keep is cold, the well is blocked, the memorial wall is empty and the company must decide who gets the first dry bed.

**Foreshadowing:** a missing line in the record refers to a duty owed by the Houses, not a right held by the Crown.

### Act I — Cold Hearth
**Dramatic question:** Can a company protect people whose needs exceed its strength?

**Scenario A: The Last Convoy**
- Sallow Crossing has enough winter stores for one final convoy. The settlement’s steward wants the grain kept for residents; a road captain argues that the neighbouring hamlets will starve without it.
- The player investigates the storehouse, road conditions and the convoy’s manifest before committing.
- Tactical encounter: hold the bridge and escort wagons through a route with vulnerable civilians, limited movement and enemies who seek the ledger rather than a total massacre.
- Choice: divide the stores, prioritise one destination, or expose the manipulation and attempt a risky negotiated redistribution.
- Consequences: food, trust, travel safety, the steward’s standing and the future availability of local recruits or supplies.

**Scenario B: A Name on the Toll Book**
- A tollhouse records payments to the raiders. Its keeper claims the money was protection against a worse force.
- The player can arrest the keeper, protect them in exchange for testimony, or let the town judge the matter.
- The evidence links the attack to a wider effort to control records of the Covenant.
- Bram challenges the protagonist if the decision places civilians at risk for a cleaner legal outcome.

**Act turn:** the raiders were paid to take a ledger. Food theft was a cover and an opportunity, not the original objective.

**Companion beat:** Sela refuses to treat the recovered list as proof until the names and dates can be checked against living witnesses.

### Act II — Broken Seals
**Dramatic question:** What does a public truth mean when every witness is afraid?

**Scenario A: The Orrowen Hearing**
- A public hearing concerns contested woodland rights, a levy and the wording of a Covenant obligation.
- The player gathers testimony from a forester, a tenant family, a House representative and a veteran who enforced the order.
- Each account is partly true and incomplete. The player must decide which evidence can be made public without exposing a vulnerable witness.
- The hearing can produce a binding local remedy, a temporary compromise or a public rupture.

**Scenario B: The Escort That Changed Course**
- During a diplomatic escort, the party discovers that the supposed enemy supply train contains displaced families.
- A faction has used a false banner to move people past a checkpoint. The escort commander has orders to turn them back.
- Tactical encounter: protect a narrow crossing while keeping civilians moving; optional objectives reward rescuing separated families and securing proof of the unlawful order.
- Choice: obey the writ, shelter the refugees, or negotiate a temporary passage and accept a political debt.

**Act turn:** the Seven Houses do not merely disagree about the Covenant; they each hold a different edited memory of it.

**Companion beat:** Brother Wystan argues that sanctuary is an obligation even when it is inconvenient. He does not claim that sanctuary makes every later consequence disappear.

### Act III — The River Campaign
**Dramatic question:** Can territory be secured without treating its people as terrain?

**Scenario A: Three Bridges**
- A river operation has three linked objectives: secure a ford, keep a bridge usable for evacuation, and prevent a downstream storehouse from being burned.
- The player chooses the order of operations and assigns platoons or a tactical squad according to the systems actually available.
- The enemy is not a single army: local retainers, hired soldiers and frightened conscripts have different objectives and morale.
- Optional intelligence reveals that the supply route is also a refugee route.

**Scenario B: The Banner in the Reeds**
- A unit carrying a hostile banner is discovered sheltering civilians. Their commander is a deserter who took the banner to keep a pursuing force away.
- The player may expose the deception, protect the group, or use the banner as leverage in a negotiation.
- Combat can be avoided or reduced if the player secured credible testimony in Act II.

**Act turn:** military control of a road or bridge does not prove legal ownership of the land or the loyalty of its people.

**Companion beat:** Bram admits that an earlier campaign he served in called a retreating village “enemy ground” to justify abandoning it. He does not ask to be forgiven.

### Act IV — Winter of Knives
**Dramatic question:** Is loyalty still loyalty when it refuses to obey?

**Scenario A: The Quillon Archive**
- The company seeks a missing Codicil clause in an archive whose custodians disagree about whether it should be released.
- The archive is a social and investigative space first, not a dungeon with bookshelves. Access depends on prior testimony, favours and the way the company treated records in earlier acts.
- A break-in or siege may occur, but it should be one possible failure path, not the default solution.
- The player can preserve the original, publish a verified copy, or entrust it to a coalition of custodians.

**Scenario B: The Witness Who Recanted**
- A witness from the Orrowen hearing withdraws their testimony after their family is threatened.
- The player discovers the threat but cannot guarantee the family’s safety by simply winning a battle.
- The scene tests whether the company respects the witness’s agency or uses them as a tool for the cause.

**Major revelation:** Edmer concealed one clause to protect someone. The clause may have prevented an immediate injustice but also weakened the public record. The player must understand both the protection and the cost.

**Company fracture:** companions disagree over publication, timing and responsibility. The disagreement should be authored through prior choices and relationships, not a random loyalty check.

### Act V — The Kindling
**Dramatic question:** Does truth decide the future, or do people who accept responsibility for it?

**Scenario A: The Road to Aurelmont**
- Several routes lead toward the capital: a costly direct march, a diplomatic route dependent on prior settlements, or a slower route that protects food and civilian movement.
- The chosen route determines which allies can arrive, which regions are exposed and what the company must leave behind.
- Enemy forces have coherent objectives: hold the capital approaches, protect the regent’s evacuation corridor, or prevent the Codicil from reaching the public.

**Scenario B: The Three Fires**
- The final operation has simultaneous crises: a threatened civilian quarter, a besieged archive/record hall and a military command position.
- The player cannot personally solve every crisis. Earlier trust, recruited allies and logistical decisions determine which can be delegated safely.
- Tactical success should not erase political failure. A captured gate is not a settlement; a rescued archive is not a food supply.

**Maelor’s offer:** Maelor offers a genuine settlement if the protagonist surrenders personal power and accepts a limited public process for the Codicil. He believes this is the only way to prevent famine, invasion and civil war. His offer must have real benefits and real omissions. It cannot be a fake choice where every reasonable person sees through it.

**Final decision space:** the player chooses what to publish, what to protect temporarily, what institutions must be dissolved or constrained, and whether to seek personal rule, a bounded transitional office or a non-imperial settlement. The exact available endings must be authored against the game's real political systems and cannot be promised before those systems exist.

### Epilogue — The Chronicle
**Dramatic question:** What remains when the campaign stops asking the protagonist to win?

Return to Kaer Sethorn. Revisit spaces whose state reflects the campaign: the well, the oath hall, the kitchen, the memorial and the road beyond the gate. Let surviving companions tell different versions of the same events. The epilogue records:
- who survived, departed, reconciled or refused reconciliation;
- which local promises were fulfilled or left unresolved;
- what became of the Covenant and its public obligations;
- what the protagonist chose to do with authority;
- what the realm will remember—and what it may choose to forget.

A quiet scene with one companion should carry more emotional weight than a long list of victory statistics.

## 4. Regional scenario cards

These are expandable scenario seeds. Preserve the existing region names, faction identities, geography and unlock order in live data.

### Sethmarch Frontier — The Price of a Safe Road
A chain of settlements must decide whether to pay protection money, arm their own watch or accept a Crownland garrison. The player discovers that the garrison has kept the road safe in one direction while diverting grain and arresting people without hearings in another. Resolution depends on local trust and evidence, not a single boss kill.

### Orrowen Weald — The Boundary Tree
A boundary marker is found cut down. Three groups claim the old line: tenants whose fields were enclosed, foresters who maintain the paths, and a House steward whose map predates the current settlement. The player must reconstruct the boundary from living testimony, old coppice marks and a damaged survey. A battle may defend a meeting or escort surveyors; it does not magically settle ownership.

### Vessmoor — The Milepost Duel
A public duel is proposed to settle a dispute over road authority. The duel is a political performance: the actual issue concerns tolls, mounted patrols and the right to requisition horses. The player may accept the duel, expose the terms, or propose arbitration. Lady Isaure Vessmoor’s participation must be consistent with her established recruitment and character data.

### Quillon — The Archive That Remembers
An archive preserves multiple copies of the same decree, each altered in a different hand. The player must identify which copy is earliest and who had access to each version. A successful investigation changes the credibility of later testimony; it does not give the player a magical all-knowing record.

### Tarnholt — The Bell and the Bread
A sanctuary distributes food during a shortage. Its keeper refuses to turn away anyone, while a neighbouring settlement claims the distribution is drawing armed groups toward its wells. Brother Wystan must confront the gap between mercy as a principle and the logistics of keeping people alive.

### Brinegate Coast — The Beacon’s Debt
Keep the established regional chain: Brinegate harbour, Saltmere, Lantern Quay, Old Beacon Vault, Northstar Wreck and Tideglass Grotto; Captain Ilyra Venn, Maren Vey, the darkened beacon, the keeper’s strongbox and ledger, the Reef Warden, and the choice to publish the scraped Seven Flames oath or keep it private for the 60-gold patron payment.

Polish the scenario around a central question: **who is allowed to decide what a warning means?** The beacon is a public safety instrument, but its keeper’s records also expose the private arrangements that paid for its maintenance. Publishing the oath may restore public knowledge while exposing people who relied on the old arrangement to survive. Keeping it private protects a patron’s payment but leaves the coast dependent on a compromised chain of trust. Maren’s recruitment remains gated by both ledger recovery and defeating the grotto guardian, as established in the current regional storyline.

### Varakhan — The Bitter Wells
The Nine Wells Compact is fracturing as reservoirs turn bitter. The player investigates water rights, damaged channels and rival accusations. The sealed aqueduct beneath the salt flats contains a relief map of the Covenant network and a warning written by a Dunmarrow ancestor. The water crisis must have a material cause that can be investigated; do not resolve it as “the desert is cursed.”

### Nhalor — The Names Beneath Ice
An out-of-season aurora reveals old sealed doors. The player must decide whether to open a vault that contains evidence about the Covenant network but also records the dead according to northern custom. The local people are not obstacles to knowledge: their laws and memories shape what responsible access means.

### Veyrwood — The Living Boundary
A forest route is failing because old-growth paths are being cut or diverted. Ranger lodges, local settlements and extraction interests each have legitimate needs and conflicting claims. The player should negotiate stewardship, access and repair rather than simply choose “nature” or “industry.”

### Asterreach — The Storm Charter
A storm-damaged harbour depends on a charter that grants salvage rights to those who maintain warning towers. The charter’s wording excludes families who have lived on the coast for generations. The player must decide whether to preserve a technically valid but unjust arrangement, suspend it during repairs, or establish a new compact with enforceable duties.

### Ilyr — The Far Lantern
A remote lantern archive preserves testimony that was deliberately separated from the other Covenant records. Its keepers disagree about whether the testimony should be returned to the wider world. Build Ilyr’s local culture and political conflict before using it as a final exposition machine.

## 5. Companion arcs and scene principles

Use the established cast as people with conflicting needs, not as spokespeople for theme.

| Character | Core tension | Required turning point | Avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Torren Dunmarrow | Duty versus self-erasure | Learns that refusing power can be an act of responsibility, but avoiding every decision can also abandon others | Treating him as a flawless heir or assuming he is always the player avatar |
| Bram Vosk | Protection versus obedience | Challenges an order from someone he loves because he recognises its harm | A sudden betrayal or a single speech that absolves his past |
| Sela Wrenfield | Truth versus the safety of witnesses | Chooses when and how to testify, with control over her own risk | Making her a convenient exposition device |
| Brother Wystan | Faith versus institutional certainty | Defends mercy while accepting that good intentions need practical stewardship | Reducing faith to corruption or naïve goodness |
| Edmer Dunmarrow | Courage versus compromise | His concealed clause reveals both protective intent and a real moral cost | Making his secret either wholly noble or wholly villainous |
| Maelor Brennhallow | Stability versus accountability | Offers a settlement that could genuinely prevent harm but preserves too much unchecked power | A secret monster reveal, cartoon cruelty or a final monologue explaining everything |
| Captain Ilyra Venn | Public duty versus private obligation | Must decide what the coast is owed when the truth threatens the people who kept it functioning | Reducing her to a quest-giver |
| Maren Vey | Freedom of movement versus the cost of passage | A choice to join the company should express a personal stake in the coast’s future | Treating recruitment as a reward for defeating a boss alone |

**Character-scene rule:** every important conversation should contain (a) what the speaker wants now, (b) what they are unwilling to say, (c) what the player can reasonably know, and (d) a changed relationship or decision after the scene.

## 6. Choice and consequence ledger

Every major choice should be recorded in a design ledger before scripting.

| Field | Required question |
|---|---|
| Trigger | What exact evidence, action or dialogue opens the choice? |
| Options | What can the player actually do, including refusal or delay? |
| Immediate cost | What resource, risk, time or relationship is spent now? |
| Immediate benefit | Who is protected, persuaded, supplied or informed? |
| Delayed consequence | Which later scene, recruit, route, faction response or ending changes? |
| Knowledge boundary | What does the protagonist know at the moment of choice? |
| Reversibility | Can the decision be revisited? If not, why is it irreversible? |
| Feedback | How does the game show that the world remembered it? |
| Test | What deterministic test proves each branch and save/load persistence? |

Do not hide all consequences behind a single global morality score. Use named facts and bounded relationship/faction values. The same choice can improve one settlement’s trust while worsening another faction’s confidence.

### Consequence classes
- **Local:** a person, household, settlement or route changes.
- **Company:** a companion relationship, recruitment path, personal quest or internal dispute changes.
- **Political:** a House, institution, territory or diplomatic option changes.
- **Material:** food, gold, materials, safety, time or military capacity changes.
- **Historical:** testimony, public memory, legal precedent or the final chronicle changes.

At least one later scene should visibly acknowledge every major irreversible choice. Do not promise a branch that has no later expression.

## 7. Scenario construction template

Use this template for every main quest, side quest, companion quest, dungeon and regional event.

1. **Title and location**
2. **Narrative purpose:** what new understanding or emotional movement does this create?
3. **Player-facing premise:** what the player is told before accepting?
4. **Inciting incident:** what changes the status quo?
5. **Stakeholders:** named people and groups; what each wants and fears.
6. **Investigation:** evidence, contradictions, optional clues and knowledge boundaries.
7. **Playable objectives:** clear primary and optional goals.
8. **Encounter design:** why conflict occurs; enemy identity, terrain, objectives and retreat/surrender logic.
9. **Choice:** options, immediate cost and delayed effects.
10. **Resolution:** what changes in the location and in the company?
11. **Follow-up hooks:** later scene, recruit, faction, resource or chronicle callback.
12. **Failure and recovery:** fail-forward path; avoid dead-ending the campaign for one missed optional clue.
13. **Presentation:** dialogue beats, ambient details, music/silence, journal entry and memorial record.
14. **Implementation status:** designed, data-authored, scripted, tested, device-verified.

## 8. Dialogue and scene craft

### Dialogue
- Give each speaker a distinct vocabulary, sentence length and tolerance for silence.
- Let practical people speak practically. A quartermaster counts sacks; a scout names tracks; a priest asks who will be turned away; a commander asks how long the bridge can hold.
- Avoid repeated speeches about “the realm,” “destiny,” “the flames” or “the old ways.”
- Use questions to expose competing assumptions rather than to deliver a lore lecture.
- Allow disagreement to remain unresolved when the characters have not earned reconciliation.

### Environmental storytelling
Each important location should communicate three layers:
1. **Function:** what people use the place for.
2. **History:** what happened here before the player arrived.
3. **Present pressure:** what is broken, contested, scarce or changing now.

Use the existing palette and environmental details in the Lore Bible. Repeated visits should show repair, neglect, occupation, memorials, changed trade and altered rumours.

### Tactical storytelling
A battle objective must express the story:
- escort civilians rather than simply defeat all enemies;
- hold a bridge long enough for wagons to cross;
- capture a ledger without destroying it;
- protect a witness while allowing them to retreat;
- disable a signal fire before reinforcements arrive;
- break contact and withdraw when the mission is rescue, not conquest.

Do not force combat where a credible negotiation, stealth or withdrawal path has been established. Do not make every enemy fight to the death.

## 9. Continuity and production safeguards

Before approving a scenario:
- Verify all character names, pronouns, faction affiliations, recruit gates and existing relationships against live data.
- Verify every place, route, region link and unlock condition against `continents.json` and map data.
- Verify enemy IDs, battle maps, class/weapon definitions and reward references against the current data files.
- Preserve the current Brinegate Coast quest chain and its 60-gold publication choice unless a deliberate, documented revision is approved.
- Preserve the existing four-continent blueprint and its names, links and travel progression while retaining Sethmarch Frontier; the seven-continent atlas is an expansion, not a replacement.
- Keep the Chronicle of Houses locked until the full required ending sequence is complete: campaign completion, becoming the last Emperor, formal dissolution of the Empire, late-life epilogue and natural old-age death.
- Do not introduce playable dynasty management, marriage or child simulation during the main campaign.
- Mark all unimplemented story branches as designed rather than playable.
- Keep the game fully offline and use existing Godot, JSON and save architecture.

### Acceptance criteria for a production-ready scenario
A scenario is ready for implementation only when:
- its dramatic question is clear in one sentence;
- each major stakeholder has a credible motive;
- the player has enough information to understand the choice, even if outcomes remain uncertain;
- objectives are readable and the encounter is motivated by the story;
- at least one consequence is visible later;
- optional clues enrich the story but do not create an unavoidable softlock;
- dialogue does not contradict character knowledge or voice;
- all data references exist and are validated;
- save/load preserves the branch state;
- the scene can be completed and tested offline on the target mobile layout.

## 10. Current scope statement

This bible is a **polished narrative design specification**, not a claim that all scenarios, cinematics, dialogue, branching logic, regional maps or political endings are implemented. The first implementation target should be the opening-hour sequence—Prologue, Kaer Sethorn arrival, The Last Convoy and the Toll Book investigation—because it establishes the game’s emotional contract, core company cast, settlement consequences and mystery structure. Build and test that vertical slice before expanding the entire continent.
