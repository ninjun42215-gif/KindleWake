# KindleWake — Scenario and Narrative Production Bible
## The Sundered Vow

**Purpose:** turn the established lore into authored, production-ready story scenarios with clear dramatic goals, player agency, tactical purpose and persistent consequences.  
**Status:** narrative design direction. These scenarios are not claims that the corresponding scenes, quests or branches are playable.  
**Canon authority:** the live character, continent, faction, territory, quest and enemy data; `LORE_BIBLE.md`; the existing `GDD.md`; and `PREMIUM_PRODUCTION_BIBLE.md`. If a discrepancy exists, flag it for deliberate resolution—do not silently retcon it.

---

## 1. Narrative contract

### The story in one sentence
A dispossessed heir gathers people abandoned by the realm, uncovers the obligations hidden beneath its founding Covenant, and must decide whether truth can become a more humane order—or merely another instrument of rule.

### What the player should feel
1. **Precarity:** the first victories are small, expensive and personal.
2. **Belonging:** Kaer Sethorn becomes a home because people repair it, argue in it and return to it.
3. **Complicity:** every faction has a reason for its choices; the player cannot remain morally untouched.
4. **Discovery:** each revelation reinterprets evidence already encountered.
5. **Responsibility:** power expands faster than certainty; the player must listen, negotiate and accept limits.
6. **Aftermath:** the ending is measured not only by who wins, but by who can safely live with the settlement.

### Five non-negotiable writing rules
- **No omniscient protagonist.** The player only knows what they have witnessed, been told or reasonably inferred.
- **No disposable civilians.** Refugees, labourers, tenants, sailors and soldiers have names, needs and conflicting views where the scene warrants it.
- **No villain-only institutions.** Institutions can protect people and still commit injustice; reformers can cause harm; opponents can be right about a danger.
- **No consequence-free virtue.** Mercy, honesty, restraint and generosity may be right, but they can still cost time, resources, allies or political leverage.
- **No false choice.** Choices must change a person’s circumstances, a relationship, a resource, a future option or the player’s understanding. Cosmetic dialogue variants must be labelled as such in implementation notes.

## 2. Mystery architecture: reveal the Covenant in layers

The Hidden Codicil should not be a single document that explains the entire plot. Treat it as a chain of testimony, physical evidence, contradictory records and living obligations.

| Layer | Evidence | Initial interpretation | Later reinterpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| The fire | A damaged household record and inconsistent witness accounts | The estate was destroyed to erase Dunmarrow evidence | Someone altered the record before the fire; the fire concealed more than one act |
| The winter stores | A ledger and paid raider testimony | Bandits attacked a vulnerable settlement | The raiders were paid to seize a ledger, while the shortage was already being manipulated |
| The public hearing | Conflicting copies of a Covenant clause | A local lord is breaking an old custom | Different Houses preserved different edits, each claiming its version prevented a greater harm |
| The river crossing | Refugees travelling under an enemy banner | A hostile supply movement | The banner is cover for civilians fleeing a forced relocation or unlawful levy |
| The Quillon archive | Edmer’s private notes and a missing clause | Edmer was a pure whistleblower | Edmer concealed one clause to protect someone; his courage and compromise coexist |
| Aurelmont | The complete Codicil and Maelor’s proposed settlement | The truth will settle the succession | The truth exposes obligations, but cannot itself feed, shelter or reconcile the realm |

**Fair-play rule:** plant at least two independent clues before a major reveal. One may be ambiguous; the other should become clear in hindsight. Never solve a mystery through a newly introduced witness who could have appeared earlier but conveniently did not.

## 3. Main campaign scenario spine

The intended structure remains a prologue, five acts and an epilogue. The sequence below sharpens the dramatic engine without asserting that every chapter is already implemented.

### Prologue — Smoke on the Wind
**Dramatic question:** What can be saved when a home is deliberately made unsafe?

**Opening image:** the Dunmarrow estate at night. Smoke obscures the family crest; the bell does not ring in its usual pattern. The player-character is not given a full explanation. They hear a familiar voice calling from one direction and see the household record exposed to the flames in another.

**Playable objectives:**
- Reach the inner yard while the fire and collapsing passages change the safe route.
- Decide whether to rescue a trapped household member, recover the damaged record, or split the few available helpers and risk both.
- Escape with Bram and the surviving witnesses before the gate is sealed.

**Choice and cost:** the rescue decision determines who survives or what evidence remains legible. Do not make the choice a disguised “good/evil” test. A rescued person may later disagree with the protagonist; a recovered record may be incomplete or misleading.

**Reversal:** the fire was not simply an attempt to kill the heir. The household record had been altered before the blaze. Someone wanted a particular version of events to survive.

**Quiet aftermath:** the survivors reach Kaer Sethorn. No triumphant speech. The keep is cold, the well is blocked, the memorial wall is empty and the company must decide who gets the first dry bed.

**Foreshadowing:** a missing line in the record refers to a duty owed by the Houses, not a right held by the Crown.

### Act I — Cold Hearth
**Dramatic question:** Can a company protect people whose needs exceed its strength?

**Scenario A: The Last Convoy**
- Sallow Crossing has enough winter stores for one final convoy. The settlement’s steward wants the grain kept for residents; a road captain argues that the neighbouring hamlets will starve without it.
- The player investigates the storehouse, road conditions and the convoy’s manifest before committing.
- Tactical encounter: hold the bridge and escort wagons through a route with vulnerable civilians, limited movement and enemies who seek the ledger rather than a total massacre.
- Choice: divide the stores, prioritise one destination, or expose the manipulation and attempt a risky negotiated redistribution.
- Consequences: food, trust, travel safety, the steward’s standing and the future availability of local recruits or supplies.

**Scenario B: A Name on the Toll Book**
- A tollhouse records payments to the raiders. Its keeper claims the money was protection against a worse force.
- The player can arrest the keeper, protect them in exchange for testimony, or let the town judge the matter.
- The evidence links the attack to a wider effort to control records of the Covenant.
- Bram challenges the protagonist if the decision places civilians at risk for a cleaner legal outcome.

**Act turn:** the raiders were paid to take a ledger. Food theft was a cover and an opportunity, not the original objective.

**Companion beat:** Sela refuses to treat the recovered list as proof until the names and dates can be checked against living witnesses.

### Act II — Broken Seals
**Dramatic question:** What does a public truth mean when every witness is afraid?

**Scenario A: The Orrowen Hearing**
- A public hearing concerns contested woodland rights, a levy and the wording of a Covenant obligation.
- The player gathers testimony from a forester, a tenant family, a House representative and a veteran who enforced the order.
- Each account is partly true and incomplete. The player must decide which evidence can be made public without exposing a vulnerable witness.
- The hearing can produce a binding local remedy, a temporary compromise or a public rupture.

**Scenario B: The Escort That Changed Course**
- During a diplomatic escort, the party discovers that the supposed enemy supply train contains displaced families.
- A faction has used a false banner to move people past a checkpoint. The escort commander has orders to turn them back.
- Tactical encounter: protect a narrow crossing while keeping civilians moving; optional objectives reward rescuing separated families and securing proof of the unlawful order.
- Choice: obey the writ, shelter the refugees, or negotiate a temporary passage and accept a political debt.

**Act turn:** the Seven Houses do not merely disagree about the Covenant; they each hold a different edited memory of it.

**Companion beat:** Brother Wystan argues that sanctuary is an obligation even when it is inconvenient. He does not claim that sanctuary makes every later consequence disappear.

### Act III — The River Campaign
**Dramatic question:** Can territory be secured without treating its people as terrain?

**Scenario A: Three Bridges**
- A river operation has three linked objectives: secure a ford, keep a bridge usable for evacuation, and prevent a downstream storehouse from being burned.
- The player chooses the order of operations and assigns platoons or a tactical squad according to the systems actually available.
- The enemy is not a single army: local retainers, hired soldiers and frightened conscripts have different objectives and morale.
- Optional intelligence reveals that the supply route is also a refugee route.

**Scenario B: The Banner in the Reeds**
- A unit carrying a hostile banner is discovered sheltering civilians. Their commander is a deserter who took the banner to keep a pursuing force away.
- The player may expose the deception, protect the group, or use the banner as leverage in a negotiation.
- Combat can be avoided or reduced if the player secured credible testimony in Act II.

**Act turn:** military control of a road or bridge does not prove legal ownership of the land or the loyalty of its people.

**Companion beat:** Bram admits that an earlier campaign he served in called a retreating village “enemy ground” to justify abandoning it. He does not ask to be forgiven.

### Act IV — Winter of Knives
**Dramatic question:** Is loyalty still loyalty when it refuses to obey?

**Scenario A: The Quillon Archive**
- The company seeks a missing Codicil clause in an archive whose custodians disagree about whether it should be released.
- The archive is a social and investigative space first, not a dungeon with bookshelves. Access depends on prior testimony, favours and the way the company treated records in earlier acts.
- A break-in or siege may occur, but it should be one possible failure path, not the default solution.
- The player can preserve the original, publish a verified copy, or entrust it to a coalition of custodians.

**Scenario B: The Witness Who Recanted**
- A witness from the Orrowen hearing withdraws their testimony after their family is threatened.
- The player discovers the threat but cannot guarantee the family’s safety by simply winning a battle.
- The scene tests whether the company respects the witness’s agency or uses them as a tool for the cause.

**Major revelation:** Edmer concealed one clause to protect someone. The clause may have prevented an immediate injustice but also weakened the public record. The player must understand both the protection and the cost.

**Company fracture:** companions disagree over publication, timing and responsibility. The disagreement should be authored through prior choices and relationships, not a random loyalty check.

### Act V — The Kindling
**Dramatic question:** Does truth decide the future, or do people who accept responsibility for it?

**Scenario A: The Road to Aurelmont**
- Several routes lead toward the capital: a costly direct march, a diplomatic route dependent on prior settlements, or a slower route that protects food and civilian movement.
- The chosen route determines which allies can arrive, which regions are exposed and what the company must leave behind.
- Enemy forces have coherent objectives: hold the capital approaches, protect the regent’s evacuation corridor, or prevent the Codicil from reaching the public.

**Scenario B: The Three Fires**
- The final operation has simultaneous crises: a threatened civilian quarter, a besieged archive/record hall and a military command position.
- The player cannot personally solve every crisis. Earlier trust, recruited allies and logistical decisions determine which can be delegated safely.
- Tactical success should not erase political failure. A captured gate is not a settlement; a rescued archive is not a food supply.

**Maelor’s offer:** Maelor offers a genuine settlement if the protagonist surrenders personal power and accepts a limited public process for the Codicil. He believes this is the only way to prevent famine, invasion and civil war. His offer must have real benefits and real omissions. It cannot be a fake choice where every reasonable person sees through it.

**Final decision space:** the player chooses what to publish, what to protect temporarily, what institutions must be dissolved or constrained, and whether to seek personal rule, a bounded transitional office or a non-imperial settlement. The exact available endings must be authored against the game's real political systems and cannot be promised before those systems exist.

### Epilogue — The Chronicle
**Dramatic question:** What remains when the campaign stops asking the protagonist to win?

Return to Kaer Sethorn. Revisit spaces whose state reflects the campaign: the well, the oath hall, the kitchen, the memorial and the road beyond the gate. Let surviving companions tell different versions of the same events. The epilogue records:
- who survived, departed, reconciled or refused reconciliation;
- which local promises were fulfilled or left unresolved;
- what became of the Covenant and its public obligations;
- what the protagonist chose to do with authority;
- what the realm will remember—and what it may choose to forget.

A quiet scene with one companion should carry more emotional weight than a long list of victory statistics.

## 4. Regional scenario cards

These are expandable scenario seeds. Preserve the existing region names, faction identities, geography and unlock order in live data.

### Sethmarch Frontier — The Price of a Safe Road
A chain of settlements must decide whether to pay protection money, arm their own watch or accept a Crownland garrison. The player discovers that the garrison has kept the road safe in one direction while diverting grain and arresting people without hearings in another. Resolution depends on local trust and evidence, not a single boss kill.

### Orrowen Weald — The Boundary Tree
A boundary marker is found cut down. Three groups claim the old line: tenants whose fields were enclosed, foresters who maintain the paths, and a House steward whose map predates the current settlement. The player must reconstruct the boundary from living testimony, old coppice marks and a damaged survey. A battle may defend a meeting or escort surveyors; it does not magically settle ownership.

### Vessmoor — The Milepost Duel
A public duel is proposed to settle a dispute over road authority. The duel is a political performance: the actual issue concerns tolls, mounted patrols and the right to requisition horses. The player may accept the duel, expose the terms, or propose arbitration. Lady Isaure Vessmoor’s participation must be consistent with her established recruitment and character data.

### Quillon — The Archive That Remembers
An archive preserves multiple copies of the same decree, each altered in a different hand. The player must identify which copy is earliest and who had access to each version. A successful investigation changes the credibility of later testimony; it does not give the player a magical all-knowing record.

### Tarnholt — The Bell and the Bread
A sanctuary distributes food during a shortage. Its keeper refuses to turn away anyone, while a neighbouring settlement claims the distribution is drawing armed groups toward its wells. Brother Wystan must confront the gap between mercy as a principle and the logistics of keeping people alive.

### Brinegate Coast — The Beacon’s Debt
Keep the established regional chain: Brinegate harbour, Saltmere, Lantern Quay, Old Beacon Vault, Northstar Wreck and Tideglass Grotto; Captain Ilyra Venn, Maren Vey, the darkened beacon, the keeper’s strongbox and ledger, the Reef Warden, and the choice to publish the scraped Seven Flames oath or keep it private for the 60-gold patron payment.

Polish the scenario around a central question: **who is allowed to decide what a warning means?** The beacon is a public safety instrument, but its keeper’s records also expose the private arrangements that paid for its maintenance. Publishing the oath may restore public knowledge while exposing people who relied on the old arrangement to survive. Keeping it private protects a patron’s payment but leaves the coast dependent on a compromised chain of trust. Maren’s recruitment remains gated by both ledger recovery and defeating the grotto guardian, as established in the current regional storyline.

### Varakhan — The Bitter Wells
The Nine Wells Compact is fracturing as reservoirs turn bitter. The player investigates water rights, damaged channels and rival accusations. The sealed aqueduct beneath the salt flats contains a relief map of the Covenant network and a warning written by a Dunmarrow ancestor. The water crisis must have a material cause that can be investigated; do not resolve it as “the desert is cursed.”

### Nhalor — The Names Beneath Ice
An out-of-season aurora reveals old sealed doors. The player must decide whether to open a vault that contains evidence about the Covenant network but also records the dead according to northern custom. The local people are not obstacles to knowledge: their laws and memories shape what responsible access means.

### Veyrwood — The Living Boundary
A forest route is failing because old-growth paths are being cut or diverted. Ranger lodges, local settlements and extraction interests each have legitimate needs and conflicting claims. The player should negotiate stewardship, access and repair rather than simply choose “nature” or “industry.”

### Asterreach — The Storm Charter
A storm-damaged harbour depends on a charter that grants salvage rights to those who maintain warning towers. The charter’s wording excludes families who have lived on the coast for generations. The player must decide whether to preserve a technically valid but unjust arrangement, suspend it during repairs, or establish a new compact with enforceable duties.

### Ilyr — The Far Lantern
A remote lantern archive preserves testimony that was deliberately separated from the other Covenant records. Its keepers disagree about whether the testimony should be returned to the wider world. Build Ilyr’s local culture and political conflict before using it as a final exposition machine.

## 5. Companion arcs and scene principles

Use the established cast as people with conflicting needs, not as spokespeople for theme.

| Character | Core tension | Required turning point | Avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Torren Dunmarrow | Duty versus self-erasure | Learns that refusing power can be an act of responsibility, but avoiding every decision can also abandon others | Treating him as a flawless heir or assuming he is always the player avatar |
| Bram Vosk | Protection versus obedience | Challenges an order from someone he loves because he recognises its harm | A sudden betrayal or a single speech that absolves his past |
| Sela Wrenfield | Truth versus the safety of witnesses | Chooses when and how to testify, with control over her own risk | Making her a convenient exposition device |
| Brother Wystan | Faith versus institutional certainty | Defends mercy while accepting that good intentions need practical stewardship | Reducing faith to corruption or naïve goodness |
| Edmer Dunmarrow | Courage versus compromise | His concealed clause reveals both protective intent and a real moral cost | Making his secret either wholly noble or wholly villainous |
| Maelor Brennhallow | Stability versus accountability | Offers a settlement that could genuinely prevent harm but preserves too much unchecked power | A secret monster reveal, cartoon cruelty or a final monologue explaining everything |
| Captain Ilyra Venn | Public duty versus private obligation | Must decide what the coast is owed when the truth threatens the people who kept it functioning | Reducing her to a quest-giver |
| Maren Vey | Freedom of movement versus the cost of passage | A choice to join the company should express a personal stake in the coast’s future | Treating recruitment as a reward for defeating a boss alone |

**Character-scene rule:** every important conversation should contain (a) what the speaker wants now, (b) what they are unwilling to say, (c) what the player can reasonably know, and (d) a changed relationship or decision after the scene.

## 6. Choice and consequence ledger

Every major choice should be recorded in a design ledger before scripting.

| Field | Required question |
|---|---|
| Trigger | What exact evidence, action or dialogue opens the choice? |
| Options | What can the player actually do, including refusal or delay? |
| Immediate cost | What resource, risk, time or relationship is spent now? |
| Immediate benefit | Who is protected, persuaded, supplied or informed? |
| Delayed consequence | Which later scene, recruit, route, faction response or ending changes? |
| Knowledge boundary | What does the protagonist know at the moment of choice? |
| Reversibility | Can the decision be revisited? If not, why is it irreversible? |
| Feedback | How does the game show that the world remembered it? |
| Test | What deterministic test proves each branch and save/load persistence? |

Do not hide all consequences behind a single global morality score. Use named facts and bounded relationship/faction values. The same choice can improve one settlement’s trust while worsening another faction’s confidence.

### Consequence classes
- **Local:** a person, household, settlement or route changes.
- **Company:** a companion relationship, recruitment path, personal quest or internal dispute changes.
- **Political:** a House, institution, territory or diplomatic option changes.
- **Material:** food, gold, materials, safety, time or military capacity changes.
- **Historical:** testimony, public memory, legal precedent or the final chronicle changes.

At least one later scene should visibly acknowledge every major irreversible choice. Do not promise a branch that has no later expression.

## 7. Scenario construction template

Use this template for every main quest, side quest, companion quest, dungeon and regional event.

1. **Title and location**
2. **Narrative purpose:** what new understanding or emotional movement does this create?
3. **Player-facing premise:** what the player is told before accepting?
4. **Inciting incident:** what changes the status quo?
5. **Stakeholders:** named people and groups; what each wants and fears.
6. **Investigation:** evidence, contradictions, optional clues and knowledge boundaries.
7. **Playable objectives:** clear primary and optional goals.
8. **Encounter design:** why conflict occurs; enemy identity, terrain, objectives and retreat/surrender logic.
9. **Choice:** options, immediate cost and delayed effects.
10. **Resolution:** what changes in the location and in the company?
11. **Follow-up hooks:** later scene, recruit, faction, resource or chronicle callback.
12. **Failure and recovery:** fail-forward path; avoid dead-ending the campaign for one missed optional clue.
13. **Presentation:** dialogue beats, ambient details, music/silence, journal entry and memorial record.
14. **Implementation status:** designed, data-authored, scripted, tested, device-verified.

## 8. Dialogue and scene craft

### Dialogue
- Give each speaker a distinct vocabulary, sentence length and tolerance for silence.
- Let practical people speak practically. A quartermaster counts sacks; a scout names tracks; a priest asks who will be turned away; a commander asks how long the bridge can hold.
- Avoid repeated speeches about “the realm,” “destiny,” “the flames” or “the old ways.”
- Use questions to expose competing assumptions rather than to deliver a lore lecture.
- Allow disagreement to remain unresolved when the characters have not earned reconciliation.

### Environmental storytelling
Each important location should communicate three layers:
1. **Function:** what people use the place for.
2. **History:** what happened here before the player arrived.
3. **Present pressure:** what is broken, contested, scarce or changing now.

Use the existing palette and environmental details in the Lore Bible. Repeated visits should show repair, neglect, occupation, memorials, changed trade and altered rumours.

### Tactical storytelling
A battle objective must express the story:
- escort civilians rather than simply defeat all enemies;
- hold a bridge long enough for wagons to cross;
- capture a ledger without destroying it;
- protect a witness while allowing them to retreat;
- disable a signal fire before reinforcements arrive;
- break contact and withdraw when the mission is rescue, not conquest.

Do not force combat where a credible negotiation, stealth or withdrawal path has been established. Do not make every enemy fight to the death.

## 9. Continuity and production safeguards

Before approving a scenario:
- Verify all character names, pronouns, faction affiliations, recruit gates and existing relationships against live data.
- Verify every place, route, region link and unlock condition against `continents.json` and map data.
- Verify enemy IDs, battle maps, class/weapon definitions and reward references against the current data files.
- Preserve the current Brinegate Coast quest chain and its 60-gold publication choice unless a deliberate, documented revision is approved.
- Preserve the existing four-continent blueprint and its names, links and travel progression while retaining Sethmarch Frontier; the seven-continent atlas is an expansion, not a replacement.
- Keep the Chronicle of Houses locked until the full required ending sequence is complete: campaign completion, becoming the last Emperor, formal dissolution of the Empire, late-life epilogue and natural old-age death.
- Do not introduce playable dynasty management, marriage or child simulation during the main campaign.
- Mark all unimplemented story branches as designed rather than playable.
- Keep the game fully offline and use existing Godot, JSON and save architecture.

### Acceptance criteria for a production-ready scenario
A scenario is ready for implementation only when:
- its dramatic question is clear in one sentence;
- each major stakeholder has a credible motive;
- the player has enough information to understand the choice, even if outcomes remain uncertain;
- objectives are readable and the encounter is motivated by the story;
- at least one consequence is visible later;
- optional clues enrich the story but do not create an unavoidable softlock;
- dialogue does not contradict character knowledge or voice;
- all data references exist and are validated;
- save/load preserves the branch state;
- the scene can be completed and tested offline on the target mobile layout.

## 10. Current scope statement

This bible is a **polished narrative design specification**, not a claim that all scenarios, cinematics, dialogue, branching logic, regional maps or political endings are implemented. The first implementation target should be the opening-hour sequence—Prologue, Kaer Sethorn arrival, The Last Convoy and the Toll Book investigation—because it establishes the game’s emotional contract, core company cast, settlement consequences and mystery structure. Build and test that vertical slice before expanding the entire continent.

# PART II — FULL CAMPAIGN STORY DEVELOPMENT
## From the burning estate to the last Emperor

**Scope:** this section expands the five-act outline into a chapter-by-chapter narrative plan, with a developed opening sequence, supporting stories, recurring NPCs, branching consequences and a defined ending. It is a production blueprint. It does not claim that these quests or cinematics are currently implemented.

## 11. Campaign-wide dramatic structure

The main campaign is one continuous story with seven regional movements. The player begins in Eldrimar, earns passage and allies through the other continents, and returns to Aurelmont with enough evidence and political support to force a public settlement.

The story must not feel like seven unrelated continent episodes. Every regional arc should answer one of three questions:
1. What obligation did the Covenant promise here?
2. Who has been carrying the cost of its failure?
3. What does this community want to preserve when the protagonist offers reform?

### The seven regional movements

| Movement | Region / continent | Story function | Covenant question |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | Eldrimar — Sethmarch, Kaer Sethorn, Orrowen, Vessmoor, Quillon, Tarnholt, Aurelmont | Survival, company, evidence and legitimacy | Who is protected when a ruler's order conflicts with a public obligation? |
| II | Thalassar — Brinegate and the wider coast | Passage, trade, warnings and maritime memory | Who controls the routes that keep isolated communities alive? |
| III | Varakhan — Nine Wells and the salt roads | Scarcity, water law and shared reserves | Is a common resource a privilege to sell or a duty to preserve? |
| IV | Nhalor — the northern holds and sealed vaults | Memory, burial law and the cost of opening the past | Who has the right to expose the dead to public history? |
| V | Veyrwood — forest settlements and ranger paths | Stewardship, boundaries and extraction | Can a realm use land without consuming the people and future that sustain it? |
| VI | Asterreach — storm harbours and charter towns | Law, disaster, salvage and unequal protection | Does a technically lawful charter remain legitimate when it excludes those it governs? |
| VII | Ilyr — the far lantern and separated testimony | Synthesis, witness and public consent | Can the whole truth be returned to the people without turning it into another weapon? |

The precise route order must follow the actual world map and unlock data. This table is the narrative dependency order, not permission to teleport between disconnected locations. If travel order changes, move the revelations with their prerequisites and preserve foreshadowing.

## 12. Prologue — Smoke on the Wind (fully developed opening)

### Opening cinematic: The wrong bell

**Scene location:** Dunmarrow estate, late night, storm wind.

Begin with sound before exposition: rain on slate, a quill scratching, then a bell sounding twice with the wrong interval. A servant pauses. A guard looks toward the east wall. The camera never gives the player a clean view of the attackers.

The heir is in a side chamber. A half-packed travel chest suggests the household expected trouble, but no one has explained why. A messenger arrives with a sealed instruction from Edmer. Before the message can be read aloud, smoke enters beneath the door.

A household attendant says: “That is not the fire bell.”

The first player control begins immediately. No opening lecture, no royal family genealogy and no explanation of the Seven Houses yet. The player must understand the emergency through movement, sound and the people they meet.

**First playable beat — Reach the inner yard**
- Teach movement, interaction and the first environmental hazard.
- A servant is trying to lift a fallen beam; a guard insists the east passage is the only route.
- The player can help the servant, follow the guard, or inspect the burning records room.
- Each route reveals a different fragment: the fire began in a locked room, the guard's orders came from outside the household, or a record page was removed before the blaze.
- Do not punish new players with a hidden timer. Pressure comes from a closing route and changing hazards, not an unexplained countdown.

**Dialogue sample — keep the exchange short**
- Attendant: “The bell is wrong.”
- Heir: “Who rang it?”
- Attendant: “No one who wants us found alive.”

### Mission 1 — The Inner Yard

**Premise:** the household is breaking apart. Bram Vosk is trying to get survivors to the inner yard while armed strangers move through the outer passage.

**Objectives**
1. Reach Bram.
2. Help at least one group of survivors cross the yard.
3. Identify the route to the old postern gate.
4. Escape with the survivors who remain.

**Encounter design:** a small, legible introductory fight. Enemies are human intruders with a clear objective: isolate the heir and stop witnesses escaping. One enemy attempts to withdraw with a satchel rather than fight to the death. The player learns basic positioning and a simple objective-based victory condition.

**Optional discoveries**
- A dropped order bears a partial seal, not a complete faction identity.
- A wounded intruder says, “We were told the room would be empty.”
- The east stair has been barred from the outside.

**Choice — the trapped steward:** a steward is pinned under a burning beam. Bram says the postern will not stay open for long. The player can attempt the rescue, order Bram to help, or leave with the group. The choice determines the steward's survival and one surviving account of the night. If rescued, the steward later contradicts one detail in the heir's memory; this is not betrayal but trauma and limited sight.

**Resolution:** the survivors reach the inner yard, but the household's formal record is missing a page. The player does not yet know whether the page burned, was stolen or was removed earlier.

### Mission 2 — The Record Room

**Premise:** the old postern route is blocked. The only alternative crosses the record room and the damaged chapel passage.

**Objectives**
1. Find a safe route through the estate.
2. Recover the household record if possible.
3. Locate the sealed instruction Edmer left for the heir.
4. Rejoin Bram before the outer gate is taken.

**Exploration beats**
- The record room has shelves labelled by year and estate duty, grounding the Dunmarrow history in mundane administration.
- One drawer is empty but not burned; the dust outline shows a document was removed.
- A page has been cut rather than torn by fire.
- The chapel wall bears a worn Seven Flames motif, but no one pauses to explain it.

**Choice — evidence or certainty:** the player can take the damaged ledger, the sealed instruction, or ask a companion to carry both and risk slowing the escape. The ledger provides names and dates; the instruction provides a personal warning but not the full truth. If the player recovers only one, the other can be found later through a credible lead, not magically restored.

**Edmer's instruction (sample text):**
> If this reaches you after the house has gone quiet, do not mistake our name for proof. Find the people who still remember what was promised. Ask what the promise cost them. Do not ask them to kneel.

The instruction does not reveal the whole Codicil or identify Maelor as the sole architect of the fire.

### Mission 3 — The Postern Road

**Premise:** the company must leave the estate before the attackers close the outer road.

**Objectives**
1. Lead survivors to the postern.
2. Keep the wounded moving.
3. Choose whether to take the ridge path or the mill track.
4. Reach the old road without abandoning the slowest survivor.

**Route choice**
- **Ridge path:** shorter, exposed and suitable for a small armed group; risks an ambush but preserves the record.
- **Mill track:** longer and safer for the wounded; the party must leave some evidence behind or entrust it to a local family.
- Neither route is the universally correct answer. Later scenes acknowledge who was protected and what was lost.

**Final prologue encounter:** a short pursuit encounter in which the objective is to break contact. The player can fight long enough to open the route, use terrain to delay pursuers or surrender a non-essential item to buy time. Victory is escape, not extermination.

**Closing scene — the road to Kaer Sethorn:** dawn reveals the keep on a distant rise. It is not presented as a grand castle. One roof has fallen in, the outer wall is cracked and smoke rises from a tenant's cooking fire. Bram asks what the heir intends to do.

The heir's answer should be player-authored or a restrained choice of tone:
- “Find out why they came.”
- “Keep these people alive.”
- “I don't know yet.”

All three lead into the next chapter without pretending the protagonist has already decided the fate of the realm.

## 13. Act I — Cold Hearth

**Act purpose:** turn survivors into a company and a ruined fortress into a home. The player learns that local trust is earned through practical work, not declarations.

### Chapter 1 — A Keep with No Welcome

**Story:** Kaer Sethorn's remaining tenants do not greet the heir as a returning lord. They have been left with leaking roofs, a blocked well and unpaid obligations. The cast arrives as guests who need to prove they will not take the last stores.

**Quest chain**
1. **The Blocked Well:** investigate the collapse, clear debris and discover that the blockage was deliberately reinforced. A missing mason's tool points to a dispute over repair wages.
2. **A Roof Before a Banner:** choose which building to repair first: infirmary, storehouse or barracks. The first upgrade changes a practical service; the other needs remain visible.
3. **The Names at the Gate:** speak to tenant families and record who is missing. One family refuses shelter because a previous Dunmarrow steward seized their winter fuel.
4. **A Table for Strangers:** host a modest meal. Dialogue depends on whether food was shared fairly and whether the player kept the well workers paid.

**Character moments**
- Bram wants the barracks repaired first because he fears another attack.
- Sela wants the missing tenant names recorded before any public claim is made.
- Wystan insists that the infirmary is not a luxury, even if it produces no soldiers.
- The player can hear all three before deciding.

**Outcome:** Kaer Sethorn gains its first level of trust. A memorial space is established for those lost at Dunmarrow. The keep is not fully restored.

### Chapter 2 — The Last Convoy

**Story:** Sallow Crossing has one remaining winter convoy. The local steward, Edrin Vale (provisional supporting NPC; confirm against live data before adding), must decide whether to send it to the neighbouring hamlets or keep it for residents. The player discovers that the shortage has been manipulated by people who want the ledger moved.

**Quest chain**
1. **The Empty Granary:** count stores, compare receipts and interview the miller.
2. **Tracks in the Frost:** follow wagon tracks to a false camp. Find evidence that the raiders knew the convoy schedule.
3. **A Debt in Salt:** negotiate with a carter who accepted payment to delay the wagons. He fears for his sister, who is being held as leverage.
4. **Hold the Crossing:** tactical escort mission. Protect wagons and civilians while enemies attempt to seize the ledger and disable the bridge.
5. **The Measure of Bread:** decide how to distribute the remaining grain and whether to expose the steward's complicity.

**Branches**
- **Open accounting:** public trust rises if the evidence is sound; the steward may lose office, but the settlement gains a precedent for transparent stores.
- **Quiet settlement:** immediate violence is avoided and the convoy moves faster; a later witness may accuse the heir of protecting the powerful.
- **Emergency seizure:** the party secures the food quickly; local trust suffers unless the player records repayment and oversight.

**Outcome:** the party learns the raiders were paid to take a ledger. Their employer's identity remains incomplete.

### Chapter 3 — A Name on the Toll Book

**Story:** a toll keeper has a list of payments made to armed escorts who later attacked the estate's supply route. The keeper claims the payments were protection money.

**Quest chain**
1. **The Tollhouse:** investigate the office without destroying the books.
2. **Three Receipts:** compare dates, weights and names with the convoy manifest.
3. **The Keeper's Bargain:** decide whether to protect the keeper in exchange for testimony, arrest them or let the town conduct a hearing.
4. **Roadside Witness:** escort a carter who can identify the payer but is unsure whether he can identify the seal.
5. **A Mark Without a Name:** discover a partial sign used by an intermediary. It is a clue, not proof of a House's guilt.

**Act I climax — The First Oath:** the company makes a practical agreement at Kaer Sethorn: no one will be compelled to swear blind loyalty; each member states what they are willing to protect and what they will not do. This is the emotional birth of the company.

## 14. Act II — Broken Seals

**Act purpose:** establish the Covenant as a contested public obligation and make the player responsible for handling testimony.

### Chapter 4 — The Boundary Tree (Orrowen)

A boundary tree has been felled and three communities claim the land around it. The dispute appears local until the oldest survey references a clause about common access to woodland during famine.

**Quest chain**
1. **The Stump:** identify the cut and establish when it happened.
2. **The Forester's Measure:** accompany a forester along old coppice marks.
3. **The Tenant's Field:** hear a family whose land was enclosed by a recent map.
4. **The Steward's Copy:** compare the House survey with the village copy.
5. **A Line on the Ground:** hold a meeting while hired men attempt to intimidate the surveyors.

**Choice:** restore the older boundary, create a shared-use corridor, or refer the dispute to a wider hearing. Each option has a different cost in land, trust and political support. The clue about the Covenant is found in a marginal note, not a magical revelation.

### Chapter 5 — The Orrowen Hearing

A public hearing tests the player's ability to distinguish evidence from reputation. The player chooses which witnesses can speak publicly, which need protection and which documents can be authenticated.

**Scene sequence**
- The forester explains the practical use of the wood.
- The tenant describes the loss of access.
- A veteran admits that the enclosure prevented illegal logging but also blocked families who relied on fallen timber.
- The House representative presents a technically valid map with a suspiciously recent seal.
- Sela challenges the dates, not the witness's character.

**Branch outcomes:** binding local remedy, temporary compromise, or public rupture. The hearing records the decision in a form that can be cited later at Aurelmont.

### Chapter 6 — The Escort That Changed Course

A diplomatic escort is ordered to return a supply train travelling under a hostile banner. The train contains displaced families, not enemy reinforcements.

**Quest chain**
1. **The Wrong Colours:** inspect the banner and wagon marks.
2. **Names in the Canvas:** discover that the cargo list contains household names.
3. **The Commander's Writ:** learn that the order is lawful under the current emergency decree.
4. **The Narrow Crossing:** protect the families while the escort commander decides whether to obey.
5. **A Road Left Open:** negotiate passage, shelter the group or turn them back.

**Act II climax:** the party secures two independent records showing that the Seven Houses have preserved different versions of the Covenant. The conflict is now about who has authority to interpret the founding compact.

## 15. Act III — The River Campaign

**Act purpose:** move from local justice to military responsibility. The player learns that winning ground and governing it are separate tasks.

### Chapter 7 — Three Bridges

A river corridor is the only route for grain and refugees. Three objectives compete for limited troops: secure a ford, keep a bridge open for evacuation and prevent a storehouse from being burned.

**Mission structure**
- Before battle, gather intelligence from ferrymen, scouts and a local quartermaster.
- Choose the operation order and assign available units.
- During battle, objectives and enemy morale change according to prior intelligence.
- After battle, decide who repairs the bridge and who controls the crossing.

A perfect tactical victory with a destroyed bridge is still a strategic failure. The debrief must make this explicit.

### Chapter 8 — The Banner in the Reeds

A hostile banner conceals a group of deserters protecting civilians. Their commander admits taking the banner to deter pursuit. The player can expose them, protect them, recruit their expertise or turn them over to the lawful commander.

**Personal scene:** Bram recognises the commander's unit markings from a campaign in which he abandoned a village under an order he did not challenge. He tells the player before the choice; this is not revealed as a surprise after the player commits.

**Branch callbacks:** if the player protects the deserters, one may later help evacuate the capital quarter. If the player turns them over, the lawful commander may provide soldiers at the finale but the displaced families will distrust the company.

### Chapter 9 — The Price of a Crossing

The river towns demand a guarantee that their bridges will not be requisitioned without compensation. The player negotiates a crossing compact with explicit limits, repair duties and a public ledger.

**Act III climax:** the compact becomes the first example of a Covenant obligation being restored in modern form. It is imperfect and locally negotiated. Aurelmont's officials call it unlawful, making the political conflict unavoidable.

## 16. Act IV — Winter of Knives

**Act purpose:** reveal Edmer's compromise, fracture the company and force the player to decide how truth should be made public.

### Chapter 10 — The Quillon Archive

The company reaches the archive with several clues but no automatic right to enter. Access depends on prior conduct: whether the player preserved records, protected witnesses and respected the Orrowen hearing.

**Quest chain**
1. **The Door of Copies:** earn access by presenting a verified chain of custody.
2. **The Three Hands:** compare three versions of the Covenant clause and identify later edits.
3. **A Missing Leaf:** reconstruct the missing clause from references in separate ledgers.
4. **The Keeper's Price:** decide what the archive can safely publish and what must remain sealed until witnesses are protected.
5. **The Night of Knives:** defend the archive or evacuate its custodians when a faction attempts to seize the records.

### Chapter 11 — The Witness Who Recanted

A witness publicly withdraws their earlier statement after threats against their family. The player discovers the threat but cannot guarantee safety by winning a battle alone.

**Choice:** protect the family and accept a delayed hearing; expose the threat and risk retaliation; or respect the witness's withdrawal and seek independent evidence. Do not punish the player with a campaign softlock for respecting the witness.

### Chapter 12 — Edmer's Last Revision

The full evidence reveals that Edmer concealed one clause to protect a person whose identity should be revealed only after the player has earned the necessary clues. The clause concerned a contested emergency power: it allowed temporary requisition in a catastrophe, but required public accounting and an end date. Edmer feared that the clause would be used to justify permanent emergency rule if published without context.

**Important:** this proposed interpretation is an expansion of the established fact that Edmer concealed a clause; it must be checked against any more specific canon already in the GDD or data before being locked.

**Company fracture:** Sela argues that withholding the clause repeats Edmer's mistake. Bram fears that publishing it will expose the family Edmer protected. Wystan argues that a truth can be published with safeguards, but cannot be made harmless. The player chooses publication timing and protection, not whether truth exists.

**Act IV climax:** the company divides responsibilities rather than breaking into arbitrary rival factions. Each companion can act independently according to trust, prior choices and their own convictions.

## 17. Act V — The Kindling

**Act purpose:** bring the seven regional obligations together and confront the difference between taking the capital and earning a settlement.

### Chapter 13 — The Road to Aurelmont

Three routes lead toward the capital. The actual map determines which are reachable.

- **The King's Road:** fastest, exposed and dependent on military allies.
- **The Pilgrim Road:** slower, dependent on sanctuary networks and local trust.
- **The River Road:** requires the crossing compact and support from the river towns.

The route changes the allies who arrive, the resources available and which communities face retaliation. No route should make the final chapter unwinnable.

### Chapter 14 — The Seven Witnesses

The company assembles witnesses or verified testimony from the regional movements. “Seven witnesses” is a thematic name, not a rigid requirement that exactly seven NPCs must survive. A region whose witness died or refused to testify can contribute an authenticated record or a public resolution instead.

Each regional contribution states:
- the original obligation at issue;
- the harm or cost created by its failure;
- the local remedy accepted by the community;
- the limits the community demands of the new settlement.

This is where regional side stories become politically meaningful. The player sees the world not as a checklist of collectible seals, but as communities with terms of their own.

### Chapter 15 — The Three Fires

Aurelmont is in crisis. Three simultaneous operations threaten the city:
1. The civilian quarter is at risk from fire and panic.
2. The archive/record hall is threatened by seizure or destruction.
3. The command gate is contested by forces loyal to different interpretations of the emergency decree.

The player cannot personally resolve every crisis. Prior companions, local allies, logistical preparation and trust determine which operations can be delegated. The chosen direct mission is playable; the others resolve from established preparation and visible capability, not a random success roll.

**Battle objectives:** rescue and evacuate civilians, preserve the archive's original records, and secure a route for surrender negotiations. Enemy soldiers have morale and surrender conditions. The player is not rewarded for killing routed or disarmed opponents.

### Chapter 16 — The Last Audience

The player meets Maelor in a chamber where the founding Covenant was once publicly recited. Maelor presents the strongest version of his case:
- the realm is vulnerable to foreign intervention;
- the Houses will weaponise the Codicil;
- regional compacts may fail when a famine crosses borders;
- the army may split if no recognised authority remains;
- a public reckoning may produce revenge rather than justice.

The player can answer with evidence from the regions, not a single speech option. If the player ignored a region, its absence is felt. If the player made a promise and broke it, Maelor or a witness can cite that failure.

**The confrontation:** Maelor refuses to accept an unlimited return to the old Covenant order. He offers a controlled settlement with himself as permanent arbiter. The player must reject the concentration of unchecked power without pretending the dangers he named have vanished.

The final conflict can become a battle, a negotiated surrender, a public tribunal or a combination depending on accumulated support and prior choices. Even a negotiated resolution must have a playable climax and clear stakes.

## 18. The ending — The Last Emperor

### The fixed historical threshold

The main campaign's canonical end-state is that the protagonist becomes the **last Emperor of Eldrimar's imperial order**. This is not a promise of a triumphant restored empire. The crown is accepted as a temporary legal instrument to stop the immediate collapse and make the dissolution binding across the institutions that still recognise imperial authority.

The protagonist then formally abolishes the Empire and establishes the **Concord of Free Realms** (the post-imperial compact already defined in the Lore Bible). The Concord is not an empire with a different name: it has no hereditary imperial throne, no permanent central sovereign and no automatic right to levy every realm. Its limited shared offices exist only under the compact's ratified duties, review and withdrawal procedures.

The player's choices determine how the transition is achieved, what protections are secured, which allies sign, which regions remain outside the Concord and what unresolved tensions are inherited by the next age. The required historical sequence remains:
1. The campaign reaches its resolution.
2. The protagonist is recognised and crowned as the last Emperor.
3. The protagonist publicly dissolves the imperial office and its coercive claims.
4. The transition/late-life epilogue plays out.
5. The protagonist eventually dies naturally of old age.
6. Only after that death does Chronicle of Houses unlock.

No premature dynasty mode, marriage simulation, children or hereditary title management is available during the main campaign. The founder's later death is a separate, time-passed event and cannot be replaced by a battle death, assassination or scripted shortcut.

### Ending variations — what changes

The sequence above is fixed for the Chronicle gate; the *quality and shape* of the settlement are not fixed.

| Ending dimension | Earned by | Epilogue expression |
|---|---|---|
| Bread and shelter | Fair distribution, repaired routes, functioning local stores | Fewer famine warnings; named settlements remember who kept the roads open |
| Law and testimony | Protected witnesses, authenticated records, public hearings | The Concord begins with enforceable appeal and public accounting |
| Regional consent | Completed regional compacts and respected local terms | More realms sign; others negotiate autonomy or remain outside |
| Military restraint | Surrender terms, civilian protection, compensated requisition | Smaller post-war reprisals and more veterans willing to serve as wardens |
| Company trust | Kept promises, companion quests and acknowledged disagreements | Companions choose whether to remain, depart, reconcile or criticise the settlement |
| Institutional reform | Transparent treasury, limited emergency powers, oversight | The Concord has stronger checks, but implementation is slower and more politically fragile |
| Truth and memory | Publication choices, memorials and recovered records | Public histories preserve contradictions rather than erase them |

### Four epilogue settlement profiles

These are distinct political outcomes within the same Last Emperor → dissolution sequence, not four unrelated game-over screens.

**A. The Open Concord**
- Most regional compacts are ratified.
- The Codicil is published with witness protections and a public archive.
- Local appeal, shared famine reserves and limits on requisition become enforceable founding articles.
- The transition is slow and contentious; the protagonist must accept that the Concord cannot command every realm.

**B. The Guarded Concord**
- The immediate crisis is contained, but some regions refuse full ratification.
- The Codicil is published in stages with temporary safeguards.
- The new compact has stronger emergency coordination but strict expiry and review clauses.
- Stability is better in the short term; distrust persists where testimony was delayed or withheld.

**C. The Wounded Concord**
- The Empire is dissolved, but civil conflict, broken promises or missing witnesses leave several realms outside the compact.
- Local communities preserve their own agreements and may later join through negotiation.
- The player has prevented permanent imperial rule but cannot claim to have healed the realm.
- The chronicle remembers losses honestly.

**D. The Narrow Concord**
- Only a smaller coalition signs the initial compact.
- The protagonist relinquishes imperial authority anyway, refusing to use the crown to force universal consent.
- The new order is vulnerable and may face future disputes, but it begins without an imperial throne.
- This is a legitimate ending, not a hidden failure state.

No profile should be described as a simple good/evil ending. The game should show who benefits, who remains exposed and what the protagonist could not repair.

### Final scene — The empty throne

The last imperial ceremony takes place in the public hall. The throne is not destroyed in a spectacle; it is removed from the place where law is made. The imperial seal is broken or placed in a public archive according to the settlement chosen. The protagonist reads the dissolution in plain language, with the regional witnesses or their records present.

A companion asks what should be written after the protagonist's name.

The answer is not a new title. The chronicle records the office held, the office relinquished and the promises kept or broken.

The final image returns to Kaer Sethorn: the repaired well, a meal shared by people who once refused the gate, and the memorial with names added in different hands. The keep is not a palace. It is a place where people can disagree and still expect the door to open.

### Late-life coda — A life after the crown

After the campaign's political ending, the player enters a bounded late-life epilogue rather than immediately starting the dynasty simulation. This period should show the practical handover of institutions, unresolved correspondence, companions aging or departing, and the protagonist's chosen private life. It may be represented through a small number of authored time-skip scenes and decisions, not a full family-management system.

The founder's death is a quiet, natural old-age event. The scene should be dignified and grounded, not a surprise assassination or battle. The founder record preserves age, date, final title and the chosen settlement profile. The Chronicle of Houses opens only after this event has been recorded and validated.

## 19. Side stories that reinforce the main narrative

Side quests should be optional in access but not disposable in meaning. Their rewards can be modest; their consequences should make the world feel inhabited.

### The Bellmaker's Promise
A bellmaker refuses a lucrative contract because the buyer wants a warning bell tuned to sound like a routine hour. The player investigates who would benefit from a false alarm. Resolution can protect the town's warning system, expose a corrupt official or create a debt with the bellmaker. Later, the Aurelmont evacuation uses the warning pattern if the player earned the bellmaker's trust.

### The Names We Carry
A refugee child keeps a list of people separated on the road. The list is incomplete and some names are misspelled. The player can spend time tracing families, ask a sanctuary to shelter the child, or prioritise the convoy. Later scenes acknowledge the names found; never turn the list into a perfect collectible checklist.

### The Quiet Levy
A village has paid its levy twice because two authorities each claim the right to collect. The player can return the excess, negotiate a credit against future dues or expose the duplicate order. The resolution teaches that the same household can be subject to overlapping institutions.

### The Veteran's Boots
A veteran sells his boots to buy medicine for a former enemy. The player can help him find work, challenge the apothecary's price or leave him to his own decision. Bram's dialogue changes if the player has treated surrendered soldiers humanely.

### The Orchard Without a Fence
Two families share an orchard but disagree about who must repair the boundary wall. A storm destroys part of the crop. The quest tests mediation and practical cooperation, not combat. The outcome may change local food supply and a future refugee shelter.

### A Map of Small Roads
A courier asks the company to map minor tracks omitted from the official road register. These paths are used by healers, peddlers and families avoiding unlawful tolls. The map can later create a safer evacuation route, but only if the player shared it with the people who maintain it.

### The Unclaimed Standard
A battlefield standard is found with no surviving unit to claim it. Several families want it buried; a commander wants it displayed as a victory trophy. The player decides how the dead are remembered. This choice changes the tone of the final military memorial.

### A Place at the Table
A former enemy asks to attend a public meal after surrender. Some survivors object. The player must listen to the people harmed and decide whether the gathering is a reconciliation event, a practical negotiation or simply not yet possible. No forced forgiveness.

## 20. Companion personal questlines

Each companion questline should contain three beats: a practical problem, a confrontation with the character's contradiction, and a changed relationship. Recruitment alone is not a completed arc.

### Bram Vosk — The Order He Kept
1. A former soldier recognises Bram from a retreat where a village was abandoned.
2. Bram admits he obeyed an order he believed was wrong because he feared his unit would be destroyed.
3. The player helps him find the surviving villagers and hear their account without demanding forgiveness.
4. In the finale, Bram can command an evacuation, hold a defensive line or refuse an unjust order according to the trust built between him and the company.

### Sela Wrenfield — The Witness's Name
1. A witness Sela once protected is found living under another name.
2. Sela wants to publish the evidence; the witness wants safety and control over their testimony.
3. The player helps create a protected record with corroboration.
4. In the finale, Sela may testify publicly, submit the record anonymously or refuse to expose the witness.

### Brother Wystan — The Open Door
1. A sanctuary is overwhelmed and must decide who can be sheltered.
2. Wystan discovers that a senior religious official has been diverting donations, but the official also kept the sanctuary supplied during a previous famine.
3. The player chooses public accountability, private restitution or a local council investigation.
4. Wystan's resolution determines whether he remains within the institution, reforms it from within or serves as an independent keeper of sanctuary.

### Ilyra Venn — The Beacon Ledger
1. Brinegate's beacon has gone dark and the keeper's records are disputed.
2. Ilyra discovers that the maintenance arrangement protected the coast but concealed payments and unequal access.
3. The player decides whether to publish the scraped Seven Flames oath or keep it private for the established 60-gold patron payment.
4. The decision affects the coast's trust, the beacon network and how Ilyra approaches the final public record. Preserve the existing quest gate: Maren Vey's recruitment requires both ledger recovery and defeating the grotto guardian.

### Maren Vey — The Tide Between
1. Maren's old crew is divided over whether to continue running dangerous passages for isolated settlements.
2. The player helps resolve a dispute over salvage, passage fees and responsibility for shipwreck survivors.
3. Maren chooses whether to join the company as a navigator, remain a coastal ally or pursue an independent duty according to the established recruitment design.
4. If recruited, her knowledge can support the sea route to a regional finale; she must not be mandatory if the player has another credible route.

### Torren Dunmarrow — The Weight of a Name
Use only if Torren is a separate companion/NPC in the current player-character implementation. If Torren is the player-character, translate these beats into internal decisions and authored scenes rather than giving the avatar an external companion quest.
1. A surviving tenant refuses to address him by his inherited title.
2. Torren must decide whether to use the name to secure a hearing or set it aside to let the tenant speak without pressure.
3. He learns that an inherited claim can open a door but cannot make the person behind it trust him.
4. His final scene is about accepting a limited duty, not proving bloodline superiority.

## 21. Branch management and fail-forward rules

A campaign this large cannot create a separate bespoke main plot for every dialogue option. Use a clear structure:
- **Hard branches:** a character survives or dies, a region signs or refuses a compact, a record is published or withheld. These require distinct later acknowledgement.
- **Soft branches:** trust, supplies, reputation and dialogue tone change while the main chapter remains shared.
- **Knowledge branches:** the player discovers optional evidence that changes how a later scene is understood or which negotiation options appear.
- **Route branches:** travel and mission order change the allies, hazards and resources encountered.

Never make one optional side quest the sole source of a required ending key. Critical revelations need at least two acquisition paths, such as an archive copy and independent witness testimony. If a recruit is unavailable, provide a non-companion way to complete the associated strategic objective.

A character death should remove their future scenes but not erase the evidence they already contributed. A missing witness may be replaced by a verified record, not a conveniently identical new witness. A failed regional compact should have a credible recovery route through negotiation, not an arbitrary repeat battle.

## 22. Chapter-level production checklist

Before a chapter is approved, produce:
- chapter synopsis and player-facing objective;
- scene list with entry/exit conditions;
- dialogue draft with character knowledge tags;
- map/encounter brief and enemy motivation;
- choice ledger and persistent flag list;
- reward/resource budget;
- companion and faction reactions;
- journal and chronicle entries;
- fail-forward and accessibility plan;
- save/load and branch tests.

### Opening vertical-slice scope

The first playable narrative release should contain:
1. Wrong Bell cinematic and first control handoff.
2. The Inner Yard mission.
3. The Record Room investigation.
4. The Postern Road escape.
5. Kaer Sethorn arrival and first keep decision.
6. The Blocked Well and A Roof Before a Banner.
7. The Last Convoy quest chain.
8. The Toll Book investigation and First Oath scene.

This slice establishes the story, core cast, settlement loop, first tactical encounter types and choice persistence. It should be polished before building every continent's full content.
